\begin{frame}{Where we left off}
\protect\phantomsection\label{where-we-left-off}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  The panel lecture rested on one claim: anything about a firm that
  \textbf{does not change} cannot explain why its outcome changed.
  Demean, and the firm effect is gone.
\item
  The price was \textbf{strict exogeneity}: today's shock must not feed
  into tomorrow's regressors.
\end{itemize}

\vspace{0.4em}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Finance is full of outcomes that depend on \textbf{their own past}.
  Leverage, payout, cash, investment --- each is where it is largely
  because of where it was last year.
\item
  Put yesterday's \(Y\) on the right-hand side and strict exogeneity is
  not merely doubtful. \textbf{It is false by construction.}
\item
  This lecture: what breaks, how badly, how to fix it --- and when the
  fix does not work.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The plan}
\protect\phantomsection\label{the-plan}
\begin{enumerate}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Why the past belongs on the right-hand side} --- and one rule
  that settles every calculation we will need.
\item
  \textbf{Two estimators, two wrong answers.} They err in opposite
  directions, which turns out to be useful.
\item
  \textbf{The fix: instruments from the past.} Anderson--Hsiao, then
  Arellano--Bond.
\item
  \textbf{Why the fix can fail}, and what people do then.
\item
  \textbf{In practice:} a case study from capital structure, the code,
  and a checklist.
\end{enumerate}

\vspace{0.4em}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Two running questions. \emph{Which comparison is this estimator
  making?} And, new this week: \emph{which way is it wrong?}
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\section{1. Why the Past Belongs on the Right-Hand
Side}\label{why-the-past-belongs-on-the-right-hand-side}

\begin{frame}{Most things in finance drift}
\protect\phantomsection\label{most-things-in-finance-drift}
They do not jump to a target. They close part of the gap each year.

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\toprule
Leverage & firms close only part of the gap to target each year \\
Payout & managers smooth dividends, and cut only as a last resort \\
Cash & drawn down quickly, rebuilt slowly \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\[
Y_{it} \;=\; \rho\,Y_{i,t-1} + \delta D_{it} + u_i + \varepsilon_{it}, \qquad |\rho| < 1
\]

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \(\rho\) is \textbf{not a nuisance parameter --- it is the economics.}
  \(1-\rho\) is the share of the gap closed per year; a shock has
  half-life \(\ln(0.5)/\ln\rho\).
\item
  \(\rho = 0.6\): close \(40\%\) a year, half-life \(1.4\) years.
  \(\rho = 0.9\): half-life \(6.6\) years. \textbf{Those are different
  theories of the firm}, and \(\hat\rho\) is supposed to tell them
  apart.
\item
  \(\delta\) is still the treatment effect, but it is now the
  \textbf{impact} effect. The long-run effect is \(\delta/(1-\rho)\) ---
  so getting \(\rho\) wrong gets \emph{that} wrong too.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What a firm effect does to a series}
\protect\phantomsection\label{what-a-firm-effect-does-to-a-series}
\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=0.78\linewidth,height=\textheight,keepaspectratio]{06b_dynamic_panel_files/figure-beamer/two-firms-1.pdf}
\end{center}

Two firms, same \(\rho = 0.6\), same shocks in distribution ---
different \(u_i\). Back-substitute the model into itself: \[
Y_{it} \;=\; \frac{u_i}{1-\rho} \;+\; \delta\sum_{j\ge0}\rho^{\,j}D_{i,t-j} \;+\; \sum_{j\ge0}\rho^{\,j}\varepsilon_{i,t-j}
\]

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  The firm effect shifts the \textbf{level} --- not the volatility, not
  the persistence. The series look alike and sit at different heights.
\item
  \textbf{Remember this picture.} An estimator that cannot tell
  \emph{high because it was high last year} from \emph{high because this
  firm is simply high} will get \(\rho\) wrong. Both usual estimators
  fail exactly here.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{One rule settles every calculation}
\protect\phantomsection\label{one-rule-settles-every-calculation}
Read the back-substitution again: \(Y_{is}\) is assembled from \(u_i\)
and from shocks \textbf{dated \(s\) and earlier} --- nothing later. If
\(\varepsilon_{it}\) is serially uncorrelated and unrelated to \(u_i\),
that gives one rule: \[
\boxed{\;\mathbb{E}[Y_{is}\,\varepsilon_{it}] = 0 \;\;\text{whenever}\;\; t > s,
\qquad
\mathbb{E}[Y_{is}\,\varepsilon_{is}] = \sigma^2_\varepsilon\;}
\]

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  In words: \textbf{a later shock has nothing to do with an earlier
  \(Y\)}; the same-date shock enters \(Y\) with coefficient one.
\item
  Every derivation in this lecture is a \textbf{date check} against this
  rule. Write out a product, look at the two dates, and either it is
  zero or it is \(\sigma^2_\varepsilon\).
\item
  One clause is doing quiet work: \emph{serially uncorrelated}. If
  \(\varepsilon_{i,t-1}\) predicted \(\varepsilon_{it}\), a later shock
  \emph{would} be related to an earlier \(Y\). We will come back to that
  clause --- it is the identifying assumption of the whole enterprise.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The problem you cannot assume away}
\protect\phantomsection\label{the-problem-you-cannot-assume-away}
Lag the model one period and the difficulty is on the page: \[
Y_{i,t-1} \;=\; \rho\,Y_{i,t-2} + \delta D_{i,t-1} + \underbrace{u_i}_{\text{\textbf{also in today's error}}} + \;\varepsilon_{i,t-1}
\]

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Every endogenous regressor so far was endogenous because of something
  we \textbf{failed to measure}. This one is endogenous \textbf{by
  construction} --- you can read it straight off the model.
\item
  \textbf{\(Y_{i,t-1}\) contains \(u_i\)}, and so does the error. Pooled
  OLS is biased.
\item
  \textbf{Strict exogeneity is impossible.} \(\varepsilon_{it}\) moves
  \(Y_{it}\), and \(Y_{it}\) \emph{is} the regressor next period. The
  assumption fixed effects needs is contradicted by the model itself.
\item
  So pooled OLS fails and fixed effects fails. The useful part, which
  takes the next section to show: \textbf{they fail in opposite
  directions.}
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\section{2. Two Estimators, Two Wrong
Answers}\label{two-estimators-two-wrong-answers}

\begin{frame}{Why OLS reads heterogeneity as persistence}
\protect\phantomsection\label{why-ols-reads-heterogeneity-as-persistence}
Strip the model to the bone. Set \(\rho = 0\) --- \textbf{no persistence
whatsoever}, only permanent differences between firms: \[
Y_{it} \;=\; u_i + \varepsilon_{it}
\]

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  A high-\(u_i\) firm has a high \(Y\) this year \textbf{and} had a high
  \(Y\) last year. Not because the past caused the present --- because
  the firm is simply like that.
\item
  Pooled OLS cannot tell those apart. It sees \(Y\) following \(Y\) and
  calls it persistence: \[
  \operatorname{plim} \hat\rho_{\text{OLS}} \;=\; \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(Y_{it}, Y_{i,t-1})}{\operatorname{Var}(Y_{i,t-1})} \;=\; \frac{\sigma^2_u}{\sigma^2_u + \sigma^2_\varepsilon} \;>\; 0 \;=\; \rho
  \]
\item
  With \(\sigma_u = \sigma_\varepsilon = 1\) that is \(0.5\). Simulated
  with \(T=5\) years and \(100{,}000\) firms, pooled OLS returns
  \(\hat\rho = \mathbf{0.501}\) --- when the truth is \textbf{zero}.
\item
  \textbf{And it does not improve with \(T\).} More years of the same
  firm being itself is more of the same evidence.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why demeaning manufactures mean reversion}
\protect\phantomsection\label{why-demeaning-manufactures-mean-reversion}
Fixed effects removes \(u_i\) by subtracting the firm mean --- and the
firm mean contains the errors. \[
\underbrace{Y_{i,t-1} - \bar Y_{i,-1}}_{\text{regressor}}
\qquad\text{regressed on}\qquad
\underbrace{\varepsilon_{it} - \bar\varepsilon_{i}}_{\text{error}}
\]

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \(\varepsilon_{i,t-1}\) pushes the \textbf{regressor up} --- it is
  part of \(Y_{i,t-1}\). It also pushes \(\bar\varepsilon_i\) up, and
  \(\bar\varepsilon_i\) is \textbf{subtracted} from the error. The two
  sides are forced apart mechanically: \[
  \operatorname{Cov}\big(Y_{i,t-1},\, -\bar\varepsilon_i\big) \;<\; 0, \qquad \text{of order } \sigma^2_\varepsilon/T
  \]
\item
  So within a firm the data look \textbf{more mean-reverting than they
  are}, and \(\hat\rho\) is biased \textbf{down}. This is
  \textbf{Nickell (1981) bias}.
\item
  \textbf{The same \(\rho = 0\) experiment, now estimated with fixed
  effects:} \(\hat\rho_{\text{FE}} = \mathbf{-0.251}\), almost exactly
  \(-1/(T-1)\). Pooled OLS invented persistence; demeaning invents
  \textbf{mean reversion}.
\item
  The sting: this happens \textbf{even if \(u_i\) does not exist} ---
  demeaning alone does it. And it fades only at rate \(1/T\).
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{A derivation you can do at the board: \(T = 3\)}
\protect\phantomsection\label{a-derivation-you-can-do-at-the-board-t-3}
Take the smallest panel that admits the model: \(T = 3\) years,
\textbf{one differenced observation per firm}. Then
\(\hat\rho_{\text{FE}}\) is exactly least squares on
\(\Delta Y_{i3} = \rho\,\Delta Y_{i2} + \Delta\varepsilon_{i3}\), and \[
\operatorname{plim}(\hat\rho_{\text{FE}} - \rho) \;=\; \frac{\mathbb{E}[\Delta Y_{i2}\,\Delta\varepsilon_{i3}]}{\mathbb{E}[(\Delta Y_{i2})^2]}
\]

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Numerator, by the rule.} Expand
  \((Y_{i2} - Y_{i1})(\varepsilon_{i3} - \varepsilon_{i2})\) into four
  products and check dates. Three pair a \(Y\) with a \emph{later}
  shock: zero. One pairs \(Y_{i2}\) with its \emph{own} shock
  \(\varepsilon_{i2}\), with a minus sign: \(\;-\sigma^2_\varepsilon\).
\item
  \textbf{Denominator, from the picture.} \(u_i\) cancels in a
  difference, so only the \(\sum\rho^j\varepsilon\) part matters, with
  variance \(\sigma^2_\varepsilon/(1-\rho^2)\) and one-year
  autocorrelation \(\rho\). A difference of two such terms has variance
  \(2(1-\rho)\cdot\sigma^2_\varepsilon/(1-\rho^2) = 2\sigma^2_\varepsilon/(1+\rho)\).
  \[
  \operatorname{plim}(\hat\rho_{\text{FE}} - \rho) \;=\; \frac{-\sigma^2_\varepsilon}{2\sigma^2_\varepsilon/(1+\rho)} \;=\; -\,\frac{1+\rho}{2}
  \]
\item
  \(-1/2\) at \(\rho=0\), approaching \(-1\) as \(\rho\to1\).
  \textbf{For every \(\rho<1\) the plim of \(\hat\rho_{\text{FE}}\) is
  negative}: a firm whose leverage is genuinely \(90\%\) persistent is
  reported as mean-reverting. (Hansen 2022, §17.37.)
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Watching both biases at once}
\protect\phantomsection\label{watching-both-biases-at-once}
Simulate \(Y_{it} = \rho Y_{i,t-1} + u_i + \varepsilon_{it}\) with
\(\rho = 0.6\) and \(4{,}000\) firms, varying the number of years \(T\).

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{lcccc}
\toprule
Years per firm & $T=3$ & $T=5$ & $T=10$ & $T=30$ \\
\midrule
Pooled OLS \quad(biased \textbf{up}) & $0.926$ & $0.919$ & $0.920$ & $0.921$ \\
Fixed effects \quad(biased \textbf{down}) & $-0.181$ & $0.148$ & $0.399$ & $0.543$ \\
\quad \emph{Nickell (1981) plim, for comparison} & \emph{-0.200} & \emph{0.153} & \emph{0.401} & \emph{0.542} \\
\midrule
Truth & $0.600$ & $0.600$ & $0.600$ & $0.600$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{The OLS row never moves.} Its bias confuses heterogeneity with
  persistence, and more years cannot resolve it.
\item
  \textbf{At \(T = 3\) fixed effects reports a negative number} for a
  process whose true persistence is \(+0.6\) --- exactly what
  \(-(1+\rho)/2\) predicted.
\item
  The simulated FE row tracks Nickell's plim throughout, so this is
  bias, not noise.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Does it fade with \(T\)? Slowly.}
\protect\phantomsection\label{does-it-fade-with-t-slowly.}
\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth,height=\textheight,keepaspectratio]{06b_dynamic_panel_files/figure-beamer/nickell-plot-1.pdf}
\end{center}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  The bias is \(O(1/T)\): doubling the panel halves it. Finance panels
  are typically \(10\)--\(20\) years, and often unbalanced with much
  shorter firm histories.
\item
  At \(T = 30\) the bias is still \(-0.058\) at \(\rho = 0.6\) and
  \(-0.080\) at \(\rho = 0.9\). \textbf{The more persistent the series,
  the slower the bias leaves.}
\item
  Hansen's verdict is blunt: \emph{fixed effects should not be used for
  models with lagged dependent variables} \textbf{even when \(T\) is
  large.}
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Two wrongs that bound the right answer}
\protect\phantomsection\label{two-wrongs-that-bound-the-right-answer}
Neither estimator is consistent. But you know the \textbf{sign of each
bias}, so together they fence the truth in: \[
\hat\rho_{\text{FE}} \;<\; \rho \;<\; \hat\rho_{\text{OLS}}
\]

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  In the table: \(0.15 < 0.60 < 0.92\) at \(T=5\). The bracket is wide,
  and \textbf{honest}. A single number from either estimator would not
  be.
\item
  \textbf{This is the standard first move in dynamic panel work} (Bond
  2002). Run OLS and fixed effects \emph{before} anything clever, and
  keep the two numbers. They are the goalposts.
\item
  \textbf{Any estimator you trust must land between them.} We will build
  one in the next section; if it comes out above
  \(\hat\rho_{\text{OLS}}\) or below \(\hat\rho_{\text{FE}}\), you have
  learned something is wrong before interpreting a single coefficient.
\item
  A bracket is not a confidence interval. It is a statement about
  \textbf{bias}, and says nothing about sampling noise.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Bracketing, the other kind}
\protect\phantomsection\label{bracketing-the-other-kind}
The same trick works when you are unsure \textbf{which model} is right
--- lagged \(Y\), or fixed effects --- and what you care about is
\(\delta\).

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
If the truth is & and you estimate & then $\hat\delta$ is \\
\midrule
the lagged model & fixed effects      & too \textbf{high} \\
the fixed effects model & the lagged model & too \textbf{low} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\vspace{-0.2em}
\footnotesize

As written, for \(\delta > 0\). If \(\delta < 0\) the two directions
swap --- the bracket still holds, but read it the other way round.
(Angrist and Pischke 2009, §§5.3--5.4.) \normalsize

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Run both and report the bracket rather than picking the specification
  you prefer. It is an honest statement of what the data support.
\item
  \textbf{If the bracket excludes every sensible value --- stop.} The
  model is wrong in some other way, or finite-sample bias is severe.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\section{3. The Fix: Instruments from the
Past}\label{the-fix-instruments-from-the-past}

\begin{frame}{The fix, in one idea: Anderson--Hsiao}
\protect\phantomsection\label{the-fix-in-one-idea-andersonhsiao}
Difference the model to kill \(u_i\): \[
\Delta Y_{it} \;=\; \rho\,\Delta Y_{i,t-1} + \delta\,\Delta D_{it} + \Delta\varepsilon_{it}
\]

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  It also \textbf{leaves a new problem behind}: \(Y_{i,t-1}\) contains
  \(\varepsilon_{i,t-1}\), and so does \(\Delta\varepsilon_{it}\).
  Regressor and error share a shock, and you can say exactly how much:
  \[
  \mathbb{E}\big[\Delta Y_{i,t-1}\,\Delta\varepsilon_{it}\big] \;=\; -\,\sigma^2_\varepsilon
  \]
\item
  \textbf{So go back one more lag.} \(Y_{i,t-2}\) is \textbf{far enough
  back to be clean} --- it was already determined before either
  \(\varepsilon_{i,t-1}\) or \(\varepsilon_{it}\) happened --- yet
  \textbf{close enough to still predict} \(\Delta Y_{i,t-1}\): \[
  \mathbb{E}\big[Y_{i,t-2}\,\Delta\varepsilon_{it}\big] \;=\; 0
  \]
\item
  That is \textbf{Anderson--Hsiao (1982)}: IV on the differenced
  equation using \(Y_{i,t-2}\). It needs \(T \ge 3\).
\item
  Note what has happened. The lagged outcome caused the problem, and a
  \emph{longer} lag of the same variable solves it --- no new data, only
  the history you already have.
\item
  Those two lines carry the section. \textbf{The next two slides are the
  arithmetic behind them.}
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why differencing alone fails: four terms, one survivor}
\protect\phantomsection\label{why-differencing-alone-fails-four-terms-one-survivor}
Expand
\(\mathbb{E}\big[(Y_{i,t-1}-Y_{i,t-2})(\varepsilon_{it}-\varepsilon_{i,t-1})\big]\)
and check the dates against the rule:

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
Term & Shock dated & Value \\
\midrule
$+\,\mathbb{E}[Y_{i,t-1}\varepsilon_{it}]$     & after the $Y$ & $0$ \\
$-\,\mathbb{E}[Y_{i,t-1}\varepsilon_{i,t-1}]$  & \textbf{same as the }$\boldsymbol{Y}$ & $\boldsymbol{-\sigma^2_\varepsilon}$ \\
$-\,\mathbb{E}[Y_{i,t-2}\varepsilon_{it}]$     & after & $0$ \\
$+\,\mathbb{E}[Y_{i,t-2}\varepsilon_{i,t-1}]$  & after & $0$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  One term survives --- the firm's \textbf{own shock}, which pushes the
  regressor up and is \emph{subtracted} out of the error. Exactly
  \(-\sigma^2_\varepsilon\), shrinking in neither \(N\), \(T\), nor
  \(\rho\).
\item
  This is the same calculation as the \(T=3\) numerator two sections
  ago. Differencing is not a fix; it is where the fixed-effects bias
  \textbf{comes from}.
\item
  Compare the regressor to a hypothetical: if we could use \(Y_{i,t-2}\)
  in place of \(\Delta Y_{i,t-1}\), the surviving row would read
  ``after'', and the whole table would be zero. That is the next slide.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why \(Y_{i,t-2}\) is clean --- and still useful}
\protect\phantomsection\label{why-y_it-2-is-clean-and-still-useful}
The same rule, applied twice: \[
\mathbb{E}\big[Y_{i,t-2}\,\Delta\varepsilon_{it}\big]
= \underbrace{\mathbb{E}[Y_{i,t-2}\,\varepsilon_{it}]}_{t\,>\,t-2\;\Rightarrow\;0}
\;-\; \underbrace{\mathbb{E}[Y_{i,t-2}\,\varepsilon_{i,t-1}]}_{t-1\,>\,t-2\;\Rightarrow\;0}
\;=\; 0
\]

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Both shocks are now in the instrument's future, so both terms die. The
  only term that ever survived was the same-date one, and stepping back
  a single period is precisely what removes it. \textbf{The difference
  between a broken regressor and a valid instrument is one date.}
\item
  \textbf{Relevance, from the same picture.} \(u_i\) cancels inside
  \(\Delta Y_{i,t-1}\), so only the \(\sum\rho^j\varepsilon\) part of
  \(Y_{i,t-2}\) can covary with it: \[
  \operatorname{Cov}\big(Y_{i,t-2},\,\Delta Y_{i,t-1}\big) \;=\; (\rho-1)\,\frac{\sigma^2_\varepsilon}{1-\rho^2} \;=\; -\,\frac{\sigma^2_\varepsilon}{1+\rho} \;\neq\; 0
  \]
\item
  So the instrument is relevant and the IV is identified. Keep both
  numbers. The first-stage coefficient is that covariance over
  \(\operatorname{Var}(Y_{i,t-2}) = \sigma^2_u/(1-\rho)^2 + \sigma^2_\varepsilon/(1-\rho^2)\)
  --- and \textbf{that ratio is where the fix falls apart} (Section 4).
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Two assumptions doing all the work}
\protect\phantomsection\label{two-assumptions-doing-all-the-work}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Validity.} \(\mathbb{E}[Y_{i,t-2}\Delta\varepsilon_{it}] = 0\)
  holds \emph{only} because \(\varepsilon_{it}\) is serially
  uncorrelated --- the quiet clause in the rule. Which is to say,
  \textbf{only because the dynamics are correctly specified}. If the
  truth is AR(2) and you fit AR(1), the leftover dynamics land in
  \(\varepsilon_{it}\); now \(\varepsilon_{i,t-2}\) (inside
  \(Y_{i,t-2}\)) predicts \(\varepsilon_{i,t-1}\), and the instrument is
  contaminated.
\item
  So the lag length is not a specification detail you tune at the end.
  It is the \textbf{identifying assumption}. It is testable, and we will
  test it.
\item
  \textbf{Relevance.} On paper it holds --- we showed
  \(\operatorname{Cov}(Y_{i,t-2},\Delta Y_{i,t-1}) = -\sigma^2_\varepsilon/(1+\rho) \neq 0\).
  What matters in practice is the first-stage \emph{coefficient}, and
  that collapses in exactly the cases finance cares about.
\item
  The same two assumptions, applied to \(D\): if \(D\) is
  \textbf{strictly exogenous}, \(\Delta D_{it}\) is its own instrument.
  If \(D\) is only \textbf{predetermined} --- responds to past shocks
  but not to today's --- then \(D_{i,t-1}, D_{i,t-2},\dots\) are valid
  instruments for \(\Delta D_{it}\), by the same date check.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Arellano--Bond: use every lag you have}
\protect\phantomsection\label{arellanobond-use-every-lag-you-have}
Anderson--Hsiao uses one moment condition out of many. If
\(\varepsilon_{it}\) is serially uncorrelated, then at date \(t\)
\textbf{every} \(Y\) dated \(t-2\) or earlier passes the date check
(Holtz-Eakin, Newey and Rosen 1988; Arellano and Bond 1991):

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
Differenced equation at & valid instruments for $\Delta Y_{i,t-1}$ & how many \\
\midrule
$t = 3$ & $Y_{i1}$ & 1 \\
$t = 4$ & $Y_{i1},\, Y_{i2}$ & 2 \\
$t = 5$ & $Y_{i1},\, Y_{i2},\, Y_{i3}$ & 3 \\
$\;\;\vdots$ & & \\
$t = T$ & $Y_{i1},\, \dots,\, Y_{i,T-2}$ & $T-2$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Each period gets its own block: a \textbf{block-diagonal} instrument
  matrix with \((T-1)(T-2)/2\) columns. Overidentified once \(T > 3\),
  so the estimator is \textbf{GMM} rather than IV.
\item
  \textbf{Gain:} efficiency and stability --- one-instrument
  Anderson--Hsiao can be very noisy. \textbf{Cost:} the count grows with
  \(T^2\): \(91\) instruments at \(T = 15\), \(406\) at \(T = 30\). Next
  slide.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Too many instruments}
\protect\phantomsection\label{too-many-instruments}
Roodman (2009): a GMM estimator with many instruments relative to \(N\)
is quietly a bad estimator.

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{It overfits the endogenous regressor.} With enough
  instruments, the first stage can reproduce \(\Delta Y_{i,t-1}\) almost
  exactly --- including the part correlated with
  \(\Delta\varepsilon_{it}\). The estimator drifts back toward the
  biased OLS answer it was built to avoid.
\item
  \textbf{It disarms the overidentification test.} The Hansen \(J\)
  statistic becomes too weak to reject anything; a \(p\)-value of
  \(0.99\) is a warning sign, not a clean bill of health.
\item
  \textbf{Two remedies, both standard.} \emph{Limit the lag depth} ---
  use \(Y_{i,t-2}\) and \(Y_{i,t-3}\) only, say. \emph{Collapse} ---
  replace the block-diagonal matrix by one column per lag, so the count
  grows with \(T\) instead of \(T^2\).
\item
  Rule of thumb: keep the instrument count \textbf{well below \(N\)},
  and \textbf{report it}. A referee who cannot see the count cannot
  evaluate the estimate.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[fragile]{One step, two steps, and the standard errors}
\protect\phantomsection\label{one-step-two-steps-and-the-standard-errors}
GMM needs a weighting matrix, and the choice matters in small samples.

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{One-step} uses a weighting matrix built from the known
  structure of \(\Delta\varepsilon\) --- adjacent differences share a
  shock, so they are correlated \(-\tfrac12\). It does not depend on
  residuals, so it is \emph{less random}. Fine under homoskedasticity.
\item
  \textbf{Two-step} re-weights using first-step residuals.
  Asymptotically efficient under heteroskedasticity --- but its standard
  errors are \textbf{badly biased downward} in finite samples, because
  they ignore the noise in the estimated weights.
\item
  \textbf{Windmeijer (2005)} corrects the two-step standard errors, and
  the correction is large. Stata applies it by default with
  \texttt{twostep\ vce(robust)}. Never report uncorrected two-step
  standard errors.
\item
  Practical reading: with a few hundred firms and a dozen years,
  one-step with robust standard errors and two-step with Windmeijer
  should agree. If they do not, something is weak.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Diagnostics you must report}
\protect\phantomsection\label{diagnostics-you-must-report}
Every one of these is a check on an assumption the estimator cannot
verify for itself.

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{AR(1) in the differenced residuals --- expected.}
  \(\Delta\varepsilon_{it}\) and \(\Delta\varepsilon_{i,t-1}\) share
  \(\varepsilon_{i,t-1}\), so first-order correlation of about
  \(-\tfrac12\) is a sign the model is behaving.
\item
  \textbf{AR(2) in the differenced residuals --- must be absent.}
  \(\Delta\varepsilon_{it}\) and \(\Delta\varepsilon_{i,t-2}\) share
  nothing \emph{unless \(\varepsilon\) itself is serially correlated}.
  If it is, \(Y_{i,t-2}\) fails the date check and \textbf{every
  instrument is invalid}. This is the test of the identifying
  assumption. It is not optional.
\item
  \textbf{Hansen \(J\) test of overidentifying restrictions} --- with
  the instrument count beside it, for the reason on the previous slide.
\item
  \textbf{The bracket.} Does the estimate sit between
  \(\hat\rho_{\text{FE}}\) and \(\hat\rho_{\text{OLS}}\)? If it drifts
  toward the OLS end, suspect too many instruments; if toward the FE
  end, suspect weak ones.
\item
  \textbf{The first stage}, as for any IV. The next section explains why
  it is usually the problem.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\section{4. Why the Fix Can Fail}\label{why-the-fix-can-fail}

\begin{frame}{Weak instruments: the first stage, in closed form}
\protect\phantomsection\label{weak-instruments-the-first-stage-in-closed-form}
Blundell and Bond (1998) wrote the first stage,
\(\Delta Y_{i,t-1} = \gamma\,Y_{i,t-2} + v_{it}\), and divided the two
quantities from Section 3: \[
\begin{aligned}
\gamma \;&=\; \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(Y_{i,t-2},\Delta Y_{i,t-1})}{\operatorname{Var}(Y_{i,t-2})}
\;=\; \frac{-\sigma^2_\varepsilon/(1+\rho)}{\sigma^2_u/(1-\rho)^2 + \sigma^2_\varepsilon/(1-\rho^2)} \\[0.3em]
&=\; (\rho - 1)\,\frac{k}{k + \sigma^2_u/\sigma^2_\varepsilon},
\qquad k = \frac{1-\rho}{1+\rho}
\end{aligned}
\]

\vspace{-0.4em}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  The covariance on top never goes to zero; \textbf{the damage is all in
  the denominator.} The \emph{level} \(Y_{i,t-2}\) gets swamped by
  things that say nothing about the \emph{change} \(\Delta Y_{i,t-1}\).
\item
  \textbf{As \(\rho \to 1\)}, the level wanders without bound while
  changes stay the same size --- a random walk's level says nothing
  about its next step.
\item
  \textbf{As \(\sigma^2_u/\sigma^2_\varepsilon\) grows}, the level is
  mostly the firm's permanent height, which cancels out of the change.
  In the two-firm picture: the heights tell you nothing about the
  wiggles.
\item
  Both are the normal case in corporate finance: \textbf{persistent
  series, large firm effects.}
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{A picture of the first stage}
\protect\phantomsection\label{a-picture-of-the-first-stage}
\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth,height=\textheight,keepaspectratio]{06b_dynamic_panel_files/figure-beamer/gamma-plot-1.pdf}
\end{center}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  At \(\rho = 0.6\) with equal variances, \(\gamma = -0.08\): a usable
  first stage. At \(\rho = 0.95\), \(\gamma = -0.0013\) --- essentially
  nothing.
\item
  Leverage ratios, payout ratios and cash holdings all sit in the
  right-hand third of this plot. \textbf{The instrument is weakest
  exactly where you most need it.}
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Watching it fail}
\protect\phantomsection\label{watching-it-fail}
Same simulation design, now with four estimators. \(500\) firms,
\(T = 6\), \(200\) replications; the table reports the \textbf{median}
and the \textbf{10th--90th percentile range} across replications.

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\toprule
 & $\rho = 0.6$ & $\rho = 0.95$ \\
\midrule
Pooled OLS & $0.92$ \;{\footnotesize[$0.91$, $0.93$]} & $1.00$ \;{\footnotesize[$1.00$, $1.00$]} \\
Fixed effects & $0.23$ \;{\footnotesize[$0.20$, $0.26$]} & $0.47$ \;{\footnotesize[$0.44$, $0.50$]} \\
Anderson--Hsiao (one instrument) & $0.59$ \;{\footnotesize[$0.50$, $0.71$]} & $0.86$ \;{\footnotesize[$-0.41$, $2.16$]} \\
Arellano--Bond (all lags, one-step) & $0.58$ \;{\footnotesize[$0.51$, $0.67$]} & $0.55$ \;{\footnotesize[$0.19$, $0.93$]} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{At \(\rho = 0.6\) the fix works.} Both IV estimators sit
  inside the bracket, close to the truth; Arellano--Bond is the tighter
  of the two.
\item
  \textbf{At \(\rho = 0.95\) it does not.} Anderson--Hsiao is scattered
  across the number line. Arellano--Bond is biased \textbf{down toward
  fixed effects} with an enormous spread --- the textbook signature of
  weak instruments.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{System GMM: the idea, and its price}
\protect\phantomsection\label{system-gmm-the-idea-and-its-price}
Blundell and Bond's repair: keep the differenced equation, and
\textbf{add the levels equation back}, instrumented the other way round:
\[
Y_{it} = \rho\,Y_{i,t-1} + u_i + \varepsilon_{it}, \qquad \text{instrument } Y_{i,t-1} \text{ with } \Delta Y_{i,t-1}
\]

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Why it helps.} The change predicts the level even when the
  level cannot predict the change:
  \(\operatorname{Cov}(\Delta Y_{i,t-1}, Y_{i,t-1})\) stays bounded away
  from zero as \(\rho \to 1\). The first stage no longer dies.
\item
  \textbf{What it costs.} The levels error still contains \(u_i\), so
  you need \(\mathbb{E}[\Delta Y_{i,t-1}\,u_i] = 0\): \textbf{changes
  must be unrelated to the firm's permanent level.} That is exactly what
  the two-firm picture shows --- \emph{if both firms started at their
  own long-run levels}.
\item
  The formal name is \textbf{mean stationarity}: a firm's distance from
  its own long-run level in year \(1\) is unrelated to that level.
\item
  \textbf{When to doubt it.} Firms observed from IPO are typically
  \emph{below} long-run leverage and converging up; a panel that starts
  after a crisis has everyone off target in a correlated way. There the
  extra instruments are invalid, and system GMM rests on an assumption
  you do not believe. Say which case you are in.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[fragile]{Long panels: a different route}
\protect\phantomsection\label{long-panels-a-different-route}
When \(T\) is large the problem is milder and a different tool is
available.

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  The fixed-effects bias is \(O(1/T)\) \textbf{and its formula is known}
  (Nickell 1981). So correct it: estimate FE, compute the bias at
  \(\hat\rho\), subtract, iterate. Kiviet (1995); Bruno (2005) for
  unbalanced panels --- Stata's \texttt{xtlsdvc}. No instruments, no
  weak-instrument problem.
\item
  Hahn and Kuersteiner (2002) show the corrected estimator is efficient
  as \(N, T \to \infty\) together; Hahn, Hausman and Kuersteiner (2007)
  use \textbf{long differences} --- \(Y_{it} - Y_{i,t-k}\) --- which
  keeps the instruments strong even for persistent series.
\item
  Flannery and Hankins (2013) ran a horse race on finance-like panels
  --- short, persistent, unbalanced, with endogenous controls --- and
  found the bias-corrected FE and system GMM estimators generally
  reliable, and difference GMM frequently not.
\item
  \textbf{Rule of thumb.} \(T \le 10\) and \(\rho\) moderate:
  Arellano--Bond, with the diagnostics. \(\rho\) near one: system GMM,
  stating the initial-condition assumption. \(T \ge 20\): bias-corrected
  FE, which has fewer ways to go wrong.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\section{5. In Practice}\label{in-practice}

\begin{frame}{A case study: how fast do firms adjust leverage?}
\protect\phantomsection\label{a-case-study-how-fast-do-firms-adjust-leverage}
The partial-adjustment model is our equation with \(Y\) = leverage and
\(1-\rho\) the \textbf{speed of adjustment}. The literature's estimates:

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{llcc}
\toprule
Paper & Estimator & $\hat\rho$ & speed $1-\hat\rho$ \\
\midrule
Fama and French (2002) & pooled (Fama--MacBeth) & $\approx 0.85$ & $\approx 7$--$18\%$ a year \\
Flannery and Rangan (2006) & fixed effects & $\approx 0.66$ & $\approx 34\%$ \\
Lemmon, Roberts and Zender (2008) & system GMM & $\approx 0.75$ & $\approx 25\%$ \\
Huang and Ritter (2009) & long differencing & $\approx 0.80$ & $\approx 17$--$23\%$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  A decade of disagreement about whether firms have targets at all ---
  and \textbf{the ordering is the bracket}. Fixed effects is biased down
  in \(\rho\), so it reports the \emph{fastest} adjustment; pooled
  estimation is biased up, so the \emph{slowest}. The IV-type estimates
  sit between.
\item
  The substantive question (do firms rebalance toward targets, or does
  leverage just drift?) \textbf{turned on the bias this lecture is
  about.} A half-life of \(1.7\) years and one of \(5\) years are
  different theories of the firm.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Conclusion}
\protect\phantomsection\label{conclusion}
\begin{enumerate}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{A lagged \(Y\) is endogenous by construction}, not by
  omission. You can read the problem off the model.
\item
  \textbf{One rule does every calculation}: later shocks are unrelated
  to earlier \(Y\); the same-date shock enters with coefficient one.
  Check dates.
\item
  \textbf{OLS is biased up and fixed effects down.} Neither improves
  with \(N\); FE improves only at rate \(1/T\). Run both first ---
  \textbf{the bracket is the goalposts.}
\item
  \textbf{The fix is a longer lag of the same variable.} \(Y_{i,t-2}\)
  is one date too early to be contaminated. Arellano--Bond uses all such
  lags; cap and collapse them.
\item
  \textbf{The fix fails when the series is persistent or firm effects
  are large} --- the normal case in finance. Then: system GMM with its
  assumption stated, or bias-corrected FE if \(T\) allows.
\end{enumerate}

\vspace{0.3em}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  The question to carry out: \textbf{which way is this estimator wrong?}
  If you can say that, you can say what the truth is between.
\item
  \textbf{Next:} difference-in-differences --- when the switching has a
  \emph{date}.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}
