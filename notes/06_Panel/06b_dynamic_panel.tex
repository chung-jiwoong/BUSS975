\begin{frame}{Where we left off}
\protect\phantomsection\label{where-we-left-off}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Fixed effects rests on one idea: anything about a firm that
  \textbf{does not change} cannot explain why its outcome changed. The
  price was \textbf{strict exogeneity}: today's shock must not feed into
  tomorrow's regressors.
\item
  Leverage, payout, cash, investment all depend on \textbf{their own
  past}. Put last year's \(Y\) on the right-hand side and strict
  exogeneity is not doubtful --- it is \textbf{false by construction}.
\end{itemize}

\vspace{0.5em}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  One question runs through this lecture:
\end{itemize}

\begin{center}
\emph{Is a firm high this year because it was high last year ---\\ or because it is simply a high firm?}
\end{center}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Every estimator we meet is a different way of answering it. The two
  you already know both get it wrong, in opposite directions. That turns
  out to be useful.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The plan}
\protect\phantomsection\label{the-plan}
\begin{enumerate}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{The problem.} OLS and fixed effects both get persistence wrong
  --- and we know which way.
\item
  \textbf{The fix.} A longer lag of the same variable, used as an
  instrument.
\item
  \textbf{When the fix fails.} Persistent series, large firm effects ---
  exactly the finance case --- and the repair.
\item
  \textbf{In practice.} Leverage adjustment, the code, what to report.
\end{enumerate}
\end{frame}

\section{1. The Problem}\label{the-problem}

\begin{frame}{Most things in finance drift}
\protect\phantomsection\label{most-things-in-finance-drift}
They do not jump to a target. They close part of the gap each year. \[
Y_{it} \;=\; \rho\,Y_{i,t-1} + \delta D_{it} + u_i + \varepsilon_{it}, \qquad |\rho| < 1
\]

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \(\rho\) \textbf{is the economics.} A firm closes a fraction
  \(1-\rho\) of its gap each year; a shock has half-life
  \(\ln 0.5 / \ln\rho\) --- \(1.4\) years at \(\rho = 0.6\), \(6.6\) at
  \(\rho = 0.9\). Whether firms \emph{rebalance} toward a target or
  merely \emph{drift} is a question about \(\rho\).
\item
  \(\delta\) is now the \textbf{impact} effect; the long-run effect is
  \(\delta/(1-\rho)\). Get \(\rho\) wrong and you get that wrong too.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Two firms, one question}
\protect\phantomsection\label{two-firms-one-question}
\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=0.78\linewidth,height=\textheight,keepaspectratio]{06b_dynamic_panel_files/figure-beamer/two-firms-1.pdf}
\end{center}

Same \(\rho = 0.6\), same shocks in distribution, different \(u_i\).
Each firm wiggles around its own long-run level \(u_i/(1-\rho)\).

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  The firm effect sets the \textbf{height}. \(\rho\) sets \textbf{how
  fast a wiggle dies out}. Those are the two things an estimator must
  keep apart.
\item
  Pooled OLS reads height as persistence. Fixed effects, removing
  height, manufactures mean reversion. Next two slides.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{OLS reads height as persistence}
\protect\phantomsection\label{ols-reads-height-as-persistence}
Strip the model to the bone: set \(\rho = 0\). No persistence at all,
only firms that differ permanently. \[
Y_{it} \;=\; u_i + \varepsilon_{it}
\]

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  A high-\(u_i\) firm is high this year \textbf{and} was high last year.
  Not because the past caused the present --- the firm is simply like
  that. OLS sees \(Y\) following \(Y\): \[
  \operatorname{plim}\hat\rho_{\text{OLS}} \;=\; \frac{\sigma^2_u}{\sigma^2_u + \sigma^2_\varepsilon} \;>\; 0 = \rho
  \]
\item
  Simulated with \(\sigma_u = \sigma_\varepsilon\):
  \(\hat\rho_{\text{OLS}} = 0.50\) when the truth is \textbf{zero}.
  \textbf{Biased up}, and more years do not help --- more years of the
  firm being itself is more of the same evidence.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Fixed effects manufactures mean reversion}
\protect\phantomsection\label{fixed-effects-manufactures-mean-reversion}
Demeaning subtracts each firm's \emph{average} error from every year's
error. A lucky shock last year raised \(Y_{i,t-1}\) --- and sits inside
that average. \[
\underbrace{Y_{i,t-1} - \bar Y_{i}}_{\text{regressor: contains } +\varepsilon_{i,t-1}}
\qquad
\underbrace{\varepsilon_{it} - \bar\varepsilon_{i}}_{\text{error: contains } -\varepsilon_{i,t-1}/T}
\]

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  The year the regressor is high is a year the error has been pushed
  down. The fitted slope tilts \textbf{down}: \textbf{Nickell (1981)
  bias}, of order \(1/T\).
\item
  Same \(\rho = 0\) experiment, \(T = 5\):
  \(\hat\rho_{\text{FE}} = -0.25\), which is \(-1/(T-1)\). OLS invented
  persistence; demeaning invents reversion.
\item
  It happens \textbf{even without any firm effect}: demeaning alone does
  it.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Both biases in one picture}
\protect\phantomsection\label{both-biases-in-one-picture}
\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=0.82\linewidth,height=\textheight,keepaspectratio]{06b_dynamic_panel_files/figure-beamer/bias-T-1.pdf}
\end{center}

\(\rho = 0.6\), \(4{,}000\) firms, \(\sigma_u = \sigma_\varepsilon\).
Dotted: Nickell's formula for the fixed-effects plim.

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{OLS never moves.} Heterogeneity is not resolved by more years.
\item
  \textbf{Fixed effects heals, slowly.} At \(T = 3\) it is
  \emph{negative} for a process with persistence \(0.6\); at \(T = 30\)
  it is still short. Finance panels are \(10\)--\(20\) years. The more
  persistent the series, the slower the bias leaves.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Two wrongs that fence in the truth}
\protect\phantomsection\label{two-wrongs-that-fence-in-the-truth}
\[
\hat\rho_{\text{FE}} \;<\; \rho \;<\; \hat\rho_{\text{OLS}}
\]

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Neither is consistent, but you know the \textbf{sign} of each bias. At
  \(T = 5\) above: \(0.14 < 0.60 < 0.92\). Wide --- and honest.
\item
  \textbf{The standard first move} (Bond 2002): run both \emph{before}
  anything clever, and keep both numbers. They are the goalposts.
\item
  \textbf{Any estimator you trust must land between them.} One that
  lands outside is telling you an assumption failed --- before you
  interpret a single coefficient.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\section{2. The Fix}\label{the-fix}

\begin{frame}{Differencing kills the firm effect --- and shows the real
problem}
\protect\phantomsection\label{differencing-kills-the-firm-effect-and-shows-the-real-problem}
\[
\Delta Y_{it} \;=\; \rho\,\Delta Y_{i,t-1} + \delta\,\Delta D_{it} + \Delta\varepsilon_{it}
\]

\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth,height=\textheight,keepaspectratio]{06b_dynamic_panel_files/figure-beamer/timeline-1.pdf}
\end{center}

\vspace{-0.3em}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \(u_i\) is gone. But the regressor \(\Delta Y_{i,t-1}\) contains last
  year's shock, and so does the error \(\Delta\varepsilon_{it}\) ---
  \textbf{with a minus sign}. Differencing is not the fix; it is where
  the fixed-effects bias comes from.
\item
  \textbf{Go back one more year.} \(Y_{i,t-2}\) is built from shocks
  dated \(t-2\) and earlier: it shares nothing with the error, yet it is
  part of \(\Delta Y_{i,t-1}\), so it predicts the regressor.
\item
  That is \textbf{Anderson--Hsiao (1982)}: IV on the differenced
  equation, \(Y_{i,t-2}\) as instrument. \textbf{A broken regressor and
  a valid instrument are one date apart.}
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Arellano--Bond: use every lag you have}
\protect\phantomsection\label{arellanobond-use-every-lag-you-have}
At date \(t\), \emph{every} \(Y\) dated \(t-2\) or earlier is a valid
instrument. Use them all (Holtz-Eakin, Newey and Rosen 1988; Arellano
and Bond 1991):

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
Differenced equation at & valid instruments for $\Delta Y_{i,t-1}$ & count \\
\midrule
$t = 3$ & $Y_{i1}$ & 1 \\
$t = 4$ & $Y_{i1},\, Y_{i2}$ & 2 \\
$t = T$ & $Y_{i1},\, \dots,\, Y_{i,T-2}$ & $T-2$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \((T-1)(T-2)/2\) instruments in all, so the estimator is \textbf{GMM}.
  Gain: Anderson--Hsiao with one instrument is very noisy; this is far
  tighter.
\item
  Cost: the count grows with \(T^2\) --- \(91\) at \(T = 15\), \(406\)
  at \(T = 30\). \textbf{Too many instruments} (Roodman 2009) overfit
  the regressor, contamination included, and GMM slides back toward OLS
  on the differenced equation, which is biased \emph{down}. Hansen's
  \(J\) loses the power to object.
\item
  Remedies: \textbf{cap} the lag depth, \textbf{collapse} the instrument
  set to one column per lag. Keep the count well below \(N\), and report
  it.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What to report, and why}
\protect\phantomsection\label{what-to-report-and-why}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Windmeijer (2005) standard errors.} Two-step GMM is efficient
  but its standard errors are far too small in finite samples. One-step
  robust and two-step Windmeijer should agree; if they do not, something
  is weak.
\item
  \textbf{AR(2) test on \(\Delta\varepsilon\).} AR(1) is expected ---
  adjacent differences share \(\varepsilon_{i,t-1}\). AR(2) must be
  \emph{absent}: it appears only if \(\varepsilon\) itself is serially
  correlated, and then \(Y_{i,t-2}\) shares a shock with the error and
  \textbf{every instrument fails}. This is the test of the identifying
  assumption.
\item
  \textbf{Hansen \(J\)}, with the instrument count next to it. \(J\) is
  informative only when the count is small relative to \(N\).
\item
  \textbf{The bracket.} Below \(\hat\rho_{\text{FE}}\): weak or too many
  instruments. Above \(\hat\rho_{\text{OLS}}\): a violated assumption
  --- next section shows one.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\section{3. When the Fix Fails}\label{when-the-fix-fails}

\begin{frame}{Weak instruments: a level barely predicts a change}
\protect\phantomsection\label{weak-instruments-a-level-barely-predicts-a-change}
\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=0.7\linewidth,height=\textheight,keepaspectratio]{06b_dynamic_panel_files/figure-beamer/gamma-plot-1.pdf}
\end{center}

Each year a firm closes a fraction \(1-\rho\) of its gap to its own
long-run level: \[
\Delta Y_{i,t-1} \;=\; -(1-\rho)\,\underbrace{\big(Y_{i,t-2} - \tfrac{u_i}{1-\rho}\big)}_{\text{gap}} + \varepsilon_{i,t-1}
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
\gamma \;=\; (\rho-1)\,\frac{\operatorname{Var}(\text{gap})}{\operatorname{Var}(Y_{i,t-2})}
\]

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  The level predicts the change \textbf{only through the gap}; its
  height predicts nothing. \textbf{Two ways to die:} \(\rho \to 1\), the
  gap closes too slowly to matter; large \(\sigma^2_u\), the level is
  mostly height (Blundell and Bond 1998).
\item
  \(\gamma = -0.08\) at \(\rho = 0.6\); \(-0.001\) at \(\rho = 0.95\).
  Leverage, payout and cash live in the right-hand third. \textbf{The
  instrument is weakest exactly where you need it.}
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{System GMM: instrument the level with the change}
\protect\phantomsection\label{system-gmm-instrument-the-level-with-the-change}
Blundell and Bond (1998): keep the differenced equation, and \textbf{add
the levels equation}, instrumented the other way round: \[
Y_{it} = \rho\,Y_{i,t-1} + u_i + \varepsilon_{it}, \qquad \text{instrument } Y_{i,t-1} \text{ with } \Delta Y_{i,t-1}
\]

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Why it helps.} A level cannot predict a random walk's next
  step --- but \textbf{a firm that just rose is high}. The first-stage
  coefficient of \(Y_{i,t-1}\) on \(\Delta Y_{i,t-1}\) is \(\tfrac12\)
  for \emph{every} \(\rho\). It never dies.
\item
  \textbf{What it costs.} The levels error contains \(u_i\), so a firm's
  changes must say nothing about its permanent level:
  \(\mathbb{E}[\Delta Y_{i,t-1}\,u_i] = 0\). True in the two-firm
  picture \emph{because both firms started at their own long-run levels}
  --- \textbf{mean stationarity}.
\item
  \textbf{When to doubt it.} Firms observed from IPO start away from
  target and converge; a panel that starts after a crisis has everyone
  off target together. Then rising \emph{does} reveal a high-\(u_i\)
  firm, and the extra instruments are invalid.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Every estimator, on one line}
\protect\phantomsection\label{every-estimator-on-one-line}
\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=1\linewidth,height=\textheight,keepaspectratio]{06b_dynamic_panel_files/figure-beamer/one-line-1.pdf}
\end{center}

\scriptsize \(500\) firms, \(T = 6\), \(200\) draws. Dots: medians;
bars: 10th--90th percentiles; shaded: the bracket; dashed: the truth.
\normalsize

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{\(\rho = 0.6\):} every IV lands in the bracket, near the
  truth. \textbf{\(\rho = 0.95\):} the difference estimators collapse;
  system GMM holds (\(0.98\)).
\item
  \textbf{Firms start off target:} Arellano--Bond recovers (\(0.95\));
  system GMM lands at \(1.22\), \emph{above OLS} --- and the bracket
  catches it. \textbf{Each estimator fails exactly when its own
  assumption does.}
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[fragile]{Long panels: a different route}
\protect\phantomsection\label{long-panels-a-different-route}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Bias-corrected fixed effects.} Nickell's bias has a known
  formula, so subtract it: estimate FE, correct at \(\hat\rho\), iterate
  (Kiviet 1995; Bruno 2005; Stata \texttt{xtlsdvc}). No instruments, so
  nothing to be weak --- but the other regressors must be strictly
  exogenous, which finance controls rarely are.
\item
  \textbf{Horse race on finance-like panels} --- short, persistent,
  unbalanced (Flannery and Hankins 2013): bias-corrected FE and system
  GMM generally reliable; difference GMM frequently not.
\end{itemize}

\vspace{0.3em}

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\toprule
If & then \\
\midrule
$T \le 10$, $\rho$ moderate & Arellano--Bond, with the diagnostics \\

\end{center}

\begin{frame}{Conclusion}
\protect\phantomsection\label{conclusion}
\begin{enumerate}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{A lagged \(Y\) is endogenous by construction.} The question is
  whether a firm is high because it \emph{was} high, or because it
  \emph{is} high.
\item
  \textbf{OLS says ``was high'' too often; fixed effects too rarely.}
  Run both first: \textbf{the bracket is the goalposts.}
\item
  \textbf{The fix is a longer lag of the same variable.} \(Y_{i,t-2}\)
  is one date too early to be contaminated. Arellano--Bond uses all such
  lags; cap and collapse them.
\item
  \textbf{The fix fails when the series is persistent or firm effects
  are large} --- the normal case in finance. Then system GMM, if firms
  sit at their own long-run levels; or bias-corrected FE, if \(T\)
  allows.
\item
  \textbf{Each estimator fails exactly when its own assumption does},
  and the bracket usually shows it. Ask of every estimate: \emph{which
  way is it wrong?}
\end{enumerate}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{B1. The date rule, and the Nickell bias at \(T = 3\)}
\protect\phantomsection\label{b1.-the-date-rule-and-the-nickell-bias-at-t-3}
\(Y_{is}\) is built from \(u_i\) and shocks dated \(s\) and earlier. If
\(\varepsilon\) is serially uncorrelated and unrelated to \(u_i\): \[
\mathbb{E}[Y_{is}\,\varepsilon_{it}] = 0 \;\;\text{for } t > s, \qquad \mathbb{E}[Y_{is}\,\varepsilon_{is}] = \sigma^2_\varepsilon
\]

With \(T = 3\) there is one differenced observation per firm, so
\(\hat\rho_{\text{FE}}\) is least squares on
\(\Delta Y_{i3} = \rho\,\Delta Y_{i2} + \Delta\varepsilon_{i3}\): \[
\operatorname{plim}(\hat\rho_{\text{FE}} - \rho) = \frac{\mathbb{E}[\Delta Y_{i2}\,\Delta\varepsilon_{i3}]}{\mathbb{E}[(\Delta Y_{i2})^2]}
= \frac{-\sigma^2_\varepsilon}{2\sigma^2_\varepsilon/(1+\rho)} = -\,\frac{1+\rho}{2}
\]

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Numerator: expand
  \((Y_{i2} - Y_{i1})(\varepsilon_{i3} - \varepsilon_{i2})\). Three
  products pair a \(Y\) with a \emph{later} shock: zero. One pairs
  \(Y_{i2}\) with its own shock, with a minus sign:
  \(-\sigma^2_\varepsilon\).
\item
  Denominator: \(u_i\) cancels in a difference; what is left has
  variance \(2\sigma^2_\varepsilon/(1+\rho)\).
\item
  The plim is negative for every \(\rho < 1\). (Hansen 2022, §17.37.)
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{B2. One date apart, as a table}
\protect\phantomsection\label{b2.-one-date-apart-as-a-table}
Each cell is \(\mathbb{E}[\text{row} \times \text{column}]\); the last
column is the first minus the second.

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
 & $\varepsilon_{it}$ & $\varepsilon_{i,t-1}$ & $\Delta\varepsilon_{it}$ \\
\midrule
$Y_{i,t-1}$ & $0$ (later shock) & $\boldsymbol{\sigma^2_\varepsilon}$ (same date) & $-\sigma^2_\varepsilon$ \\
$Y_{i,t-2}$ & $0$ (later shock) & $0$ (later shock) & $0$ \\
\midrule
$\Delta Y_{i,t-1} = Y_{i,t-1} - Y_{i,t-2}$ & & & $\boldsymbol{-\sigma^2_\varepsilon}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Validity.} The regressor inherits the one same-date term; the
  instrument, one year older, has none.
\item
  \textbf{Relevance.}
  \(\operatorname{Cov}(Y_{i,t-2}, \Delta Y_{i,t-1}) = -\sigma^2_\varepsilon/(1+\rho) \ne 0\)
  --- but small when \(\rho\) is near one, which is the weak-instrument
  problem of Section 3.
\item
  \textbf{The zero row needs serially uncorrelated \(\varepsilon\).} If
  the truth is AR(2) and you fit AR(1), \(\varepsilon_{i,t-2}\) inside
  \(Y_{i,t-2}\) predicts \(\varepsilon_{i,t-1}\) and the instrument is
  invalid. That is what the AR(2) test checks.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{B5. Bracketing \(\delta\): lagged \(Y\) or fixed effects?}
\protect\phantomsection\label{b5.-bracketing-delta-lagged-y-or-fixed-effects}
When you are unsure \textbf{which model} is right --- lagged \(Y\), or
fixed effects --- and what you care about is \(\delta\), the two
estimates bracket it too.

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
If the truth is & and you estimate & then $\hat\delta$ is \\
\midrule
the lagged model & fixed effects      & too \textbf{high} \\
the fixed effects model & the lagged model & too \textbf{low} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Intuition: under the lagged model, a treated firm's \(Y_{i,t-1}\)
  already carries part of the effect, and fixed effects, ignoring it,
  attributes that part to \(D\). Under the fixed-effects model, the
  lagged \(Y\) absorbs part of the firm effect \emph{and} part of the
  treatment.
\item
  Angrist and Pischke (2009, §5.4): report both. If they agree on the
  sign and roughly on the size, the question of which model is right
  matters less than you feared.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}
