\section{1. Propensity Scores}\label{propensity-scores}

\begin{frame}{Defining the propensity score}
\protect\phantomsection\label{defining-the-propensity-score}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Propensity score}: \(e(X) = \Pr(D = 1 \mid X)\)

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    ``the conditional probability of assignment to a particular
    treatment given a vector of observed covariates''
  \end{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{Balancing property} (Rosenbaum--Rubin 1983):
  \(X \perp D \mid \Pr(D = 1 \mid X)\)

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    \(E[X \mid D=1, \Pr(D=1 \mid X)] = E[X \mid D=0, \Pr(D=1 \mid X)]\)
  \item
    Covariate distributions are similar between treatment and control
    conditional the propensity score.
  \end{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{Propensity score theorem} (Rosenbaum--Rubin 1983): if
  \((Y^1,Y^0) \indep D \mid X\), then \[
  (Y^1, Y^0) \;\indep\; D \;\mid\; e(X)
  \]

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Conditioning on a \textbf{single number} does what conditioning on
    all of \(X\) does. Ten covariates collapse to one scalar --- the
    curse of dimensionality defeated, for \emph{conditioning}.
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Checking balance: two numbers, not a \(t\)-test}
\protect\phantomsection\label{checking-balance-two-numbers-not-a-t-test}
The balancing property is testable. Report it covariate by covariate,
before and after.

\[
\text{SMD} \;=\; \frac{\bar X_1 - \bar X_0}{\sqrt{\big(s_1^2 + s_0^2\big)/2}},
\qquad\qquad
\text{VR} \;=\; \frac{s_1^2}{s_0^2}
\]

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Standardized mean difference.} Scale-free, so leverage and log
  assets are comparable. \(|\text{SMD}| < 0.1\) is good; Rubin (2001)
  treats \(0.25\) as the outer limit past which regression adjustment is
  untrustworthy.
\item
  Keep the denominator \textbf{fixed at its raw-sample value} when
  recomputing after weighting, or the yardstick moves with the thing you
  are measuring.
\item
  \textbf{Variance ratio.} Means can match while spreads do not. Rubin:
  stay inside \([0.5,\,2]\), ideally \([0.8,\,1.25]\).
\item
  \textbf{Do not use \(t\)-tests.} Balance is a property of \emph{this
  sample}, not a population hypothesis --- and \(p\) confounds imbalance
  with sample size, so pruning to a matched subsample raises it
  mechanically even as balance worsens (Imai, King and Stuart 2008).
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[fragile]{Reading the balance plot}
\protect\phantomsection\label{reading-the-balance-plot}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Left panel, the success.} Four covariates start badly
  imbalanced --- \texttt{Size} at \(0.65\), \texttt{Leverage} and
  \texttt{ROA} near \(0.49\). After weighting, every one is under
  \(0.02\). This is the plot referees want, and it is the one most
  papers stop at.
\item
  \textbf{Left panel, the trap.} \texttt{Volatility} looks
  \emph{perfect} from the start: \(\text{SMD} = -0.010\), and a
  \(t\)-test returns \(p = 0.72\). On the evidence of means, nothing to
  see.
\item
  \textbf{Right panel.} Its variance ratio is \(\mathbf{2.27}\) ---
  treated firms have more than twice the spread. Extreme volatility
  drives selection in \emph{both} tails, so the means cancel and the
  distributions do not.
\item
  \textbf{Weighting makes it worse}, \(2.27 \to \mathbf{2.83}\), because
  the fitted score never contained \(\texttt{vol}^2\). Add that one term
  and the ratio falls to \(\mathbf{1.03}\).
\item
  The lesson of this section: judge the score by \textbf{balance
  achieved, not by fit} --- and balance means the whole distribution.
  Check squares and interactions, not just levels.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Estimating the score}
\protect\phantomsection\label{estimating-the-score}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  You know the \textbf{true} score only when you ran the experiment
  yourself. Otherwise, it must be estimated:
  \(\Pr(D_i {=} 1 \mid X_i) = F(\beta X_i)\), fitted by \textbf{logit or
  probit}.
\item
  Judge the specification by \textbf{balance achieved}, not by fit. A
  score with a high pseudo-\(R^2\) but poor balance is a bad score; the
  reverse is fine.
\item
  Fit, check balance, add interactions and polynomials where balance
  fails, refit. A legitimate specification search, because it never
  looks at \(Y\) --- the whole stage is \textbf{blind to the outcome},
  which protects against fishing. Say so in the paper.
\item
  \textbf{The score creates no support that \(X\) lacks.} If a covariate
  cell is all-treated or all-control, collapsing \(X\) to a scalar hides
  that; it does not fix it.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Three things to do with the score}
\protect\phantomsection\label{three-things-to-do-with-the-score}
Once every unit has a \(\hat e(X_i)\), there are three standard uses.

\begin{enumerate}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Diagnose.} Plot \(\hat e\) separately for treated and control
  units. This is the common-support check, and it costs nothing.
\item
  \textbf{Weight.} Reweight the sample to build a pseudo-experiment ---
  inverse probability weighting.
\item
  \textbf{Match.} Pair treated to control units with similar scores,
  then difference --- propensity score matching.
\end{enumerate}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Visualizing overlap}
\protect\phantomsection\label{visualizing-overlap}
\pandocbounded{\includegraphics[keepaspectratio]{03b_propensity_scores_files/figure-beamer/unnamed-chunk-2-1.pdf}}

\vspace{-0.4em}

\footnotesize

Right panel: a treated mass at \(\hat e \approx 1\) with no controls,

\begin{frame}{This plot belongs in every such paper}
\protect\phantomsection\label{this-plot-belongs-in-every-such-paper}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Common support is verifiable, unlike unconfoundedness.
\item
  What to do when it looks like the right panel:

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    \textbf{Trim} observations with extreme scores, or impose a caliper.
    Drop units with \(\hat e \notin [0.1,\,0.9]\). A rule of thumb, but
    a defensible and widely accepted one (Crump, Hotz, Imbens and
    Mitnik, 2009).
  \item
    \textbf{Report the change in estimand.} After trimming you describe
    a subpopulation, not the original one. Say how many observations
    went and how the treated sample shifted.
  \item
    Consider retreating to the \(ATT\), which only needs overlap in one
    direction.
  \end{itemize}
\item
  What \emph{not} to do: report the estimate and stay silent. The
  regression will happily extrapolate into a region with no data and
  give you a standard error that reflects none of that.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Inverse probability weighting}
\protect\phantomsection\label{inverse-probability-weighting}
Rather than matching, \textbf{reweight}. Write \(\hat e_i\) for
\(\hat e(X_i)\) and \(\pi = \Pr(D{=}1)\).

\small

\[
\begin{aligned}
ATE \;&=\; E\!\left[Y_1 - Y_0\right] = E\!\left[\frac{D\,Y}{e(X)}\right] - E\!\left[\frac{(1-D)\,Y}{1 - e(X)}\right] \\
ATT \;&=\; E\!\left[Y_1 - Y_0 \middle| D = 1 \right] = \frac{1}{\pi}\,\left(E\!\left[DY\right] - E\!\left[\frac{e(X)(1-D)Y}{1 - e(X)}\right]\right) \\[1em]
\widehat{ATE}_{IPW} \;&=\; \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[\frac{D_i}{\hat e_i} - \frac{1 - D_i}{1 - \hat e_i}\right] Y_i \\
\widehat{ATT}_{IPW} \;&=\; \frac{1}{n_T}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[D_i - (1 - D_i)\,\frac{\hat e_i}{1 - \hat e_i}\right] Y_i
\end{aligned}
\] \normalsize

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Both are \textbf{Horvitz--Thompson (1952)} forms.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why \emph{that} weight for the ATE?}
\protect\phantomsection\label{why-that-weight-for-the-ate}
We prove \(E\!\left[\frac{D\,Y}{e(X)}\right] = E[Y^1]\). The \(E[Y^0]\)
half is symmetric.

\scriptsize

\[
\begin{aligned}
E\!\left[ \frac{D \cdot Y}{e(X)} \right]
  &= E\!\left[ \frac{D \cdot Y^1}{e(X)} \right]
     && \text{switching equation (consistency): } D \cdot Y = D \cdot Y^1 \\
  &= E_X\!\left[ E\!\left( \frac{D \cdot Y^1}{e(X)} \,\Big|\, X \right) \right]
     && \text{law of total expectation} \\
  &= E_X\!\left[ \frac{1}{e(X)} \cdot E\big(D \cdot Y^1 \mid X\big) \right]
     && e(X) \text{ is fixed given } X \\
  &= E_X\!\left[ \frac{1}{e(X)} \cdot E(D \mid X) \cdot E\big(Y^1 \mid X\big) \right]
     && \text{unconfoundedness (exchangeability): } D \indep Y^1 \mid X \\
  &= E_X\!\left[ \frac{1}{e(X)} \cdot e(X) \cdot E\big(Y^1 \mid X\big) \right]
     && \text{by definition, } E(D \mid X) = e(X) \\
  &= E_X\big[ E(Y^1 \mid X) \big] \;=\; E[Y^1]
     && \text{cancel; law of total expectation}
\end{aligned}
\]
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why \emph{that} weight for the ATT?}
\protect\phantomsection\label{why-that-weight-for-the-att}
\scriptsize

\(E[Y^1 \mid D{=}1] = E[Y \mid D{=}1] = \frac{E[DY]}{E[D]}\). \[
\begin{aligned}
E\big[Y^0 \mid D{=}1\big]
  &= E_X\big[\, E(Y^0 \mid X, D{=}1) \,\big|\, D{=}1 \,\big]
     && \text{law of total expectation, over } f(x \mid D{=}1) \\
  &= E_X\big[\, E(Y^0 \mid X, D{=}0) \,\big|\, D{=}1 \,\big]
     && \text{unconfoundedness --- swap the arm} \\
  &= E_X\!\left[\, E(Y^0 \mid X, D{=}0)\cdot\frac{e(X)}{\pi} \,\right]
     && \text{Bayes: } f(x \mid D{=}1) = e(x)f(x)/\pi \\
  &= E_X\!\left[\, \frac{E\big[(1-D)\,Y \mid X\big]}{1 - e(X)}\cdot\frac{e(X)}{\pi} \,\right]
     && E[(1{-}D)Y \mid X] = \big(1{-}e(X)\big)E[Y^0 \mid X, D{=}0] \\
  &= E\!\left[\, \frac{(1 - D)\,Y\,e(X)}{\big(1 - e(X)\big)\,\pi} \,\right]
     && \text{law of total expectation}
\end{aligned}
\] \normalsize

Line 3: it converts an average over the \emph{treated} covariate
distribution into one over the \emph{whole} population.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The intuition: the ATE rebuilds the population}
\protect\phantomsection\label{the-intuition-the-ate-rebuilds-the-population}
Two kinds of firm. Small firms are \textbf{rarely} treated, large firms
\textbf{usually} are.

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lrrrrrr}
\toprule
Cell & $N$ & $e$ & treated & $\times\,1/e$ & controls & $\times\,1/(1-e)$ \\
\midrule
Small & $500$ & $0.2$ & $100$ & $\times 5 = \mathbf{500}$    & $400$ & $\times 1.25 = \mathbf{500}$ \\
Large & $500$ & $0.8$ & $400$ & $\times 1.25 = \mathbf{500}$ & $100$ & $\times 5 = \mathbf{500}$ \\
\midrule
Total & $1{,}000$ & & $500$ & $\mathbf{1{,}000}$ & $500$ & $\mathbf{1{,}000}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Only one small firm in five was treated, so that one must stand in for
  all five. Its weight is \(1/0.2 = 5\).
\item
  Every weighted entry comes back to \(500\): each arm is blown back up
  to the \textbf{full population}. That is the \emph{pseudo-population},
  and in it \(D \indep X\) by construction.
\item
  Before weighting, small firms are \(20\%\) of the treated sample and
  \(80\%\) of the control sample. After weighting, \(50\%\) of both ---
  the imbalance that caused the bias is gone.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{\ldots{} and the ATT reweights controls only}
\protect\phantomsection\label{and-the-att-reweights-controls-only}
The \(ATT\) does not want the population. It wants the \textbf{treated}
--- and the treated are already a sample from that distribution, so they
keep weight \(1\). Only the controls move.

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lrrrr}
\toprule
Cell & controls & $\times\ e/(1-e)$ & $=$ & treated \\
\midrule
Small & $400$ & $0.25$ & $\mathbf{100}$ & $100$ \\
Large & $100$ & $4.00$ & $\mathbf{400}$ & $400$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Why the odds?} In a cell with score \(e\) the sample splits
  \(e : (1-e)\) between the arms. Multiplying the controls by
  \(e/(1-e)\) makes their count match the treated count \emph{exactly}
  --- last column.
\item
  The \(ATE\) weight asks ``how many units like me are in the
  population?'' The \(ATT\) weight asks ``how many \emph{treated} units
  like me are there?''
\item
  Among large firms, \(100\) controls must cover \(400\) treated, so
  each carries weight \(4\). Push \(e\) toward \(1\) and that weight
  explodes: \textbf{this is where the \(ATT\) fails}, and why it needs
  overlap in one direction only, \(e(X) < 1\), while the \(ATE\) needs
  \(0 < e(X) < 1\).
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Does it work?}
\protect\phantomsection\label{does-it-work}
\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lrrrrrr}
\toprule
Cell & $e$ & $E[Y^0]$ & $E[Y^1]$ & effect & treated & controls \\
\midrule
Small & $0.2$ & $10$ & $12$ & $2$ & $100$ & $400$ \\
Large & $0.8$ & $20$ & $26$ & $6$ & $400$ & $100$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\vspace{-0.2em}
\small

\[
\begin{aligned}
\widehat{E[Y^1]} &= \tfrac{1}{1000}\!\left[\tfrac{100(12)}{0.2} + \tfrac{400(26)}{0.8}\right] = \tfrac{6000 + 13000}{1000} = 19 \\
\widehat{E[Y^0]} &= \tfrac{1}{1000}\!\left[\tfrac{400(10)}{0.8} + \tfrac{100(20)}{0.2}\right] = \tfrac{5000 + 10000}{1000} = 15 \\[0.2em]
\widehat{ATT}\; &= \underbrace{\tfrac{100(12) + 400(26)}{500}}_{\text{treated mean}\;=\;23.2} \;-\; \underbrace{\tfrac{400(10)(0.25) + 100(20)(4)}{500}}_{\text{controls}\,\times\,\text{odds}\;=\;18.0}
\end{aligned}
\] \normalsize

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Truth: \(ATE = 4\), \(ATT = 5.2\). The naive \(SDO\) is
  \(23.2 - 12 = \mathbf{11.2}\).
\item
  IPW returns \(19 - 15 = \mathbf{4}\) and
  \(23.2 - 18.0 = \mathbf{5.2}\). Both exactly right.
\item
  \(ATT > ATE\) because the effect is bigger for large firms, and large
  firms are where treatment concentrated. \textbf{Selection on gains ---
  the two estimands correctly disagree.}
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why normalize the weights?}
\protect\phantomsection\label{why-normalize-the-weights}
IPW is \textbf{sensitive to extreme propensity scores}: one observation
with \(\hat e\) near \(0\) or \(1\) gets an enormous weight and can
dominate everything else.

Four treated firms with scores \(\hat e = (0.5,\,0.5,\,0.5,\,0.25)\),
and \textbf{every one of them has \(Y = 10\)}. With \(n = 8\):

\vspace{-0.4em}
\small

\[
\widehat{E[Y^1]} = \frac{1}{8}\left[\frac{10}{0.5} + \frac{10}{0.5} + \frac{10}{0.5} + \frac{10}{0.25}\right] = \frac{20 + 20 + 20 + 40}{8} = \frac{100}{8} = 12.5
\] \normalsize

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Every number in the data is \(10\). Any sensible estimator must return
  \(10\). This one returns \(12.5\).
\item
  The reason: the weights are \(2, 2, 2, 4\) and they \textbf{sum to
  \(10\), not to \(8\)}. Dividing by \(n\) assumes they sum to \(n\) ---
  true in population, where \(E[D/e(X)] = 1\), but never exactly true in
  a sample with \(\hat e\) estimated.
\item
  Fix: \textbf{divide by the weights you actually got, not by \(n\).}
  Here \(100/10 = 10\).
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[fragile]{The normalized (Hájek) estimator}
\protect\phantomsection\label{the-normalized-huxe1jek-estimator}
Hirano and Imbens (2001) normalize the weights \textbf{by their own sum
within each group}, so the weights sum to one in each arm:

\vspace{-0.3em}
\small

\[
\widehat{ATE}_{\text{norm}} =
\underbrace{\left[\sum_{i=1}^N \frac{Y_i D_i}{\hat e_i}\right] \bigg/ \left[\sum_{i=1}^N \frac{D_i}{\hat e_i}\right]}_{\widehat{E[Y^1]}}
\;-\;
\underbrace{\left[\sum_{i=1}^N \frac{Y_i (1 - D_i)}{1 - \hat e_i}\right] \bigg/ \left[\sum_{i=1}^N \frac{1 - D_i}{1 - \hat e_i}\right]}_{\widehat{E[Y^0]}}
\] \normalsize

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Each term is now a genuine \textbf{weighted average of observed
  outcomes}, so it must fall between the smallest and largest \(Y\) in
  that arm. The unnormalized version carries no such guarantee --- which
  is exactly how it produced \(12.5\).
\item
  Same repair for the \(ATT\): divide the reweighted control sum by
  \(\sum_i (1-D_i)\frac{\hat e_i}{1-\hat e_i}\) rather than by \(n_T\).
\item
  Default in Stata's \texttt{teffects\ ipw} and R's \texttt{WeightIt}.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Propensity score matching}
\protect\phantomsection\label{propensity-score-matching}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Match each treated unit to controls with a \textbf{similar score},
  then difference.
\item
  \textbf{Caliper} matching: refuse any match farther than some
  tolerance (say \(0.001\) on the score), \emph{discarding} the treated
  unit if none qualifies. \textbf{Radius} matching: average all controls
  inside the tolerance.
\item
  Two problems with both:

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    The tolerance is \textbf{chosen by the researcher} (Huntington-Klein
    et al.~2021).
  \item
    Dropping treated units \textbf{changes the estimand} --- the \(ATT\)
    summarised the \emph{original} treated sample.
  \end{itemize}
\item
  The score is estimated, so its sampling error enters the standard
  errors (Abadie--Imbens 2016).
\item
  King \& Nielsen, 2019: Two units can share an identical score
  \(e(X_i) = e(X_j)\) despite having completely opposite covariate
  profiles \(X_i \neq X_j\). Matching purely on the scalar score can
  actually increase local covariate imbalance compared to multivariate
  distance matching (e.g., Coarsened Exact Matching).
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What the score does and does not buy}
\protect\phantomsection\label{what-the-score-does-and-does-not-buy}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  It buys \textbf{dimension reduction}, not identification. If \(X\)
  omits a confounder, \(e(X)\) omits it too.
\item
  Standard regression silently hides lack of common support through
  linear extrapolation, whereas plotting \(\hat{e}(X)\) immediately
  exposes severe positivity violations.
\item
  a high pseudo-\(R^2\) in a treatment model means covariates perfectly
  predict treatment assignment (\(e(X) \to 0\) or \(1\)). This breaks
  the positivity assumption and causes extreme IPW variance. Conversely,
  a low pseudo-\(R^2\) indicates that treatment assignment is nearly
  independent of \(X\) (resembling a randomized control trial).
\item
  IPW shifts the modeling assumption from \(E[Y \mid D, X]\) (outcome
  equation) to \(P(D = 1 \mid X)\) (treatment assignment equation).
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\section{2. Regression}\label{regression}

\begin{frame}{Regression as a conditioning strategy}
\protect\phantomsection\label{regression-as-a-conditioning-strategy}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Regression belongs in this chapter. It is not a rival to matching ---
  it is another way of executing the same substitution from Lecture 3a,
  §3, Identification Assumptions.
\item
  Under unconfoundedness and \textbf{constant} treatment effects,
  \(Y = \alpha + \beta D + X'\gamma + \varepsilon\) recovers \(\beta\),
  because unconfoundedness delivers exactly the exogeneity condition OLS
  needs: \[
  (Y^1,Y^0)\indep D \mid X \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; E[\varepsilon \mid D, X] = 0
  \]
\item
  Recall Lecture 1: the error term contains \(Y^0\). Selection bias was
  \(\mathrm{cov}(D, Y^0) \neq 0\). Conditioning on \(X\) is the attempt
  to make that covariance zero \emph{within strata}.
\item
  What regression adds that matching lacks: it \textbf{interpolates and
  extrapolates} with functional form, so empty cells do not stop it.
\item
  What it hides: it does that silently.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\protect\phantomsection\label{frischwaughlovell}

from a \textbf{bivariate} regression of residualized \(Y\) on

\begin{frame}{What FWL implies about weights}
\protect\phantomsection\label{what-fwl-implies-about-weights}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  FWL says OLS uses the residual variation in \(D\). Where is that
  variation largest? Where treatment is \textbf{least predictable}.
\item
  Formally, with \(X\) dummies, OLS weights each stratum by
  \(N_j\, p_j(1-p_j)\) --- the \textbf{conditional variance of
  treatment} within it.
\item
  So OLS gives most weight to strata where treatment is closest to a
  coin flip, and least where it is nearly deterministic.
\item
  That is a defensible \textbf{efficiency} motive: those strata carry
  the most information per observation.
\item
  It also means \textbf{OLS is not estimating the \(ATT\)} --- or the
  \(ATE\) --- when effects are heterogeneous. It estimates a
  variance-weighted average with no policy interpretation.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Regression adjustment (imputation)}
\protect\phantomsection\label{regression-adjustment-imputation}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  A more flexible use of regression that mirrors matching directly:

  \begin{enumerate}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Fit \(\hat\mu^1(x)\) on the \textbf{treated} and \(\hat\mu^0(x)\) on
    the \textbf{controls}, separately.
  \item
    For \emph{every} unit, impute both potential outcomes and average
    the difference: \[
    \widehat{ATE} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i \big( \hat\mu^1(X_i) - \hat\mu^0(X_i) \big)
    \]
  \end{enumerate}
\item
  Separate models allow the covariates to matter differently under
  treatment and control --- exactly what a single \(\beta\) on \(D\)
  forbids.
\item
  This is the same imputation logic as matching, with a model in place
  of a neighbour.
\item
  Combining it with weighting gives \textbf{doubly robust} estimators:
  consistent if \emph{either} the outcome model or the propensity model
  is right. A genuinely useful hedge, and increasingly the default in
  applied work.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Matching versus regression}
\protect\phantomsection\label{matching-versus-regression}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Both rely on the \textbf{same} identifying assumption:
  unconfoundedness. Neither creates exogenous variation.
\item
  The difference is in \emph{how they weight strata} when the effect is
  heterogeneous.

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    \textbf{Matching (\(ATT\))} weights stratum \(j\) by the number of
    \textbf{treated} units in it.
  \item
    \textbf{OLS with \(X\) dummies} weights stratum \(j\) by
    \(N_j\, p_j (1-p_j)\).
  \end{itemize}
\item
  They also differ in what they do about missing cells: matching
  refuses; regression extrapolates.
\item
  So when the two disagree, ask two questions in order: \emph{is it the
  weights, or is it the extrapolation?} Both are diagnosable.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Reading the comparison}
\protect\phantomsection\label{reading-the-comparison}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  The regression returns \(2.265\) --- exactly the variance-weighted
  average, not the \(ATT\) and not the \(ATE\).
\item
  The gap is small here. Angrist and Pischke argue it usually is, in
  practice.
\item
  But ``usually small'' is not ``zero'', and the difference is entirely
  about \textbf{which firms your estimate describes}:

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Matching describes the treated.
  \item
    OLS describes wherever treatment was most unpredictable.
  \end{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{When it matters most}: when treatment probability varies a lot
  across strata \emph{and} effects are heterogeneous. Both are typical
  in finance.
\item

  destructively, in staggered difference-in-differences.
\end{itemize}

\section{3. What Matching Cannot Do}\label{what-matching-cannot-do}

\begin{frame}{Matching creates no new variation}
\protect\phantomsection\label{matching-creates-no-new-variation}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Matching \textbf{does not introduce exogenous variation}. It
  reorganizes and reweights the data you already have, using observables
  you already measured.
\item
  Consequence: if your OLS regression was biased by endogeneity,
  \textbf{the matched estimate is biased too}.
\item
  Contrast this with the designs still to come:

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    \textbf{IV} imports variation from outside the model.
  \item
    \textbf{RDD} exploits assignment that is locally as good as random.
  \item
    \textbf{DiD} differences out fixed unobservables.
  \end{itemize}
\item
  Matching belongs to a different category. It is a \emph{conditioning
  strategy}, like regression --- not a research design.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What it cannot fix}
\protect\phantomsection\label{what-it-cannot-fix}
Matching is powerless against:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Unobserved confounders.} You cannot match on what you cannot
  measure.
\item
  \textbf{Reverse causality.} Matching on \(X\) says nothing about
  whether \(Y\) caused \(D\).
\item
  \textbf{Measurement error} in treatment or outcome.
\item
  \textbf{Bad controls.} Matching on a post-treatment variable is
  exactly as damaging as controlling for it --- Lecture 2 applies
  unchanged, and the collider \(F\) of Lecture 3a, §2, Confounders,
  Covariates, and Colliders, is the warning.
\end{itemize}

\vspace{0.4em}

\begin{center}
\emph{You cannot match on what you cannot measure.}
\end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{So what is it good for?}
\protect\phantomsection\label{so-what-is-it-good-for}
Given all that, matching still earns its place:

\begin{enumerate}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{A non-parametric benchmark.} It estimates the effect without
  assuming a functional form. If matching and OLS disagree sharply, your
  specification is doing more work than you thought.
\item
  \textbf{Common-support discipline.} Matching refuses to compare units
  with no counterpart. Regression silently extrapolates into regions
  with no data.
\item
  \textbf{Diagnostic value.} The propensity score distribution reveals
  overlap problems that a regression table hides completely.
\item
  \textbf{Robustness when the functional form is wrong.} Matching can
  outperform a misspecified regression in finite samples.
\end{enumerate}

\vspace{0.3em}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Use it as a \emph{complement} to regression and a check on it --- not
  as an identification strategy.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\section{4. Sensitivity Analysis}\label{sensitivity-analysis}

\begin{frame}{The assumption you cannot test, revisited}
\protect\phantomsection\label{the-assumption-you-cannot-test-revisited}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Two things this course has said are both true and both final:
  unconfoundedness is \textbf{untestable}, and no amount of matching
  creates variation that fixes an unobserved confounder.
\item
  Neither is the end of the conversation. \textbf{Sensitivity analysis}
  changes the question:
\end{itemize}

\begin{center}
\emph{Not} ``is my design valid?'' --- unanswerable. \quad But: \\[0.25em]
\textbf{how large would a hidden confounder have to be to overturn my conclusion?}
\end{center}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  The output is not a \(p\)-value. It is a \textbf{threshold}, plus an
  argument about whether a confounder that large is plausible in your
  setting. The argument is yours; the threshold is arithmetic.
\item
  Two families, matching the two routes of §1, Propensity Scores, and
  §2, Regression:

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    \textbf{Design-based}, for matched pairs: Rosenbaum bounds.
  \item
    \textbf{Regression-based}, for the coefficient: Oster's \(\delta\),
    and the Cinelli--Hazlett robustness value.
  \end{itemize}
\item
  Referees in finance now ask for one of these by name. Budget a table
  for it.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Rosenbaum bounds: one parameter, \(\Gamma\)}
\protect\phantomsection\label{rosenbaum-bounds-one-parameter-gamma}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Matching made two units identical in \(X\). Unconfoundedness then says
  they had \textbf{equal} probability of treatment. Suppose they did
  not.
\item
  Let \(\Gamma \geq 1\) bound how unequal. For any two units \(i, j\)
  with \(X_i = X_j\), \[
  \frac{1}{\Gamma} \;\leq\; \frac{\pi_i (1 - \pi_j)}{\pi_j (1 - \pi_i)} \;\leq\; \Gamma .
  \]
\item
  \(\Gamma = 1\) \textbf{is} unconfoundedness. \(\Gamma = 2\) says a
  hidden variable could leave one unit of the pair twice as likely, in
  odds, to be treated.
\item
  For each \(\Gamma\), compute the \textbf{worst case} over every hidden
  assignment consistent with that \(\Gamma\), and report the resulting
  bound on the \(p\)-value of the randomization test.
\item
  Then read off \(\Gamma^{*}\): the point where the upper bound crosses
  your threshold.
\item
  Note what \(\Gamma^{*}\) is \emph{not}. It is a statement about how
  much hidden bias your \textbf{design} can absorb. It never says the
  design is fine.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Reading a Rosenbaum bound}
\protect\phantomsection\label{reading-a-rosenbaum-bound}
\(200\) matched pairs; one-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test on the
within-pair differences.

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lcccccccc}
\toprule
$\Gamma$ & $1.00$ & $1.10$ & $1.20$ & $1.30$ & $\mathbf{1.40}$ & $1.50$ & $1.75$ & $2.00$ \\
\midrule
upper bound on $p$ & $0.0001$ & $0.0008$ & $0.0040$ & $0.0149$ & $\mathbf{0.0415}$ & $0.0922$ & $0.3354$ & $0.6386$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  At \(\Gamma = 1\) the result looks bulletproof: \(p = 0.0001\). It is
  not. \(\Gamma^{*} = 1.42\).
\item
  A hidden variable that shifts the odds of treatment by \(42\%\)
  between two otherwise identical units is enough to make this finding
  insignificant.
\item
  This is the standard and slightly deflating experience. Strong
  \(p\)-values routinely coexist with small \(\Gamma^{*}\), because the
  two answer different questions.
\item
  Which leaves the question the table cannot answer: \textbf{is \(1.42\)
  big?}
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Calibrating \(\Gamma\): what does \(1.42\) mean?}
\protect\phantomsection\label{calibrating-gamma-what-does-1.42-mean}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \(\Gamma\) is one number doing the work of two. Rosenbaum and Silber
  (2009) split it back apart: let \(\Lambda\) be the odds ratio linking
  the hidden variable to \textbf{treatment}, and \(\Delta\) the odds
  ratio linking it to the \textbf{outcome}. Then \[
  \Gamma \;=\; \frac{\Lambda\Delta + 1}{\Lambda + \Delta}.
  \]
\item
  Every \((\Lambda, \Delta)\) on that curve produces the same
  \(\Gamma\). At \(\Gamma^{*} = 1.42\):
\end{itemize}

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
$\Lambda$ (odds of treatment) & $2.43$ & $3.00$ & $5.00$ \\
$\Delta$ (odds of a higher outcome) & $2.43$ & $2.07$ & $1.71$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Now the question is one a \emph{finance} audience can argue about: is
  there a plausible omitted characteristic that roughly \textbf{doubles}
  the odds of treatment and \textbf{doubles} the odds of a better
  outcome? Often the honest answer is yes.
\item
  \textbf{Benchmark it.} Compute \(\Lambda\) and \(\Delta\) for your
  strongest \emph{measured} confounder. If the killer confounder must be
  twice as strong as anything you measured, that is an argument. If half
  as strong will do, so is that.
\item
  The bound is worst-case, so \(\Gamma^{*}\) understates the true
  robustness.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Oster's \(\delta\): coefficient stability, done properly}
\protect\phantomsection\label{osters-delta-coefficient-stability-done-properly}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  The informal version is in every seminar: \emph{``the coefficient
  barely moved when I added controls, so unobservables cannot matter
  much.'\,'}
\item
  That intuition is not wrong, it is \textbf{unfinished}. It ignores how
  much the controls explained. A coefficient that is stable while
  \(R^2\) barely moves has demonstrated nothing.
\item
  Altonji, Elder and Taber (2005) turned it into an argument; Oster
  (2019) turned it into an estimator. Two inputs:

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    \(\delta\) --- selection on unobservables \textbf{relative to}
    selection on observables. \(\delta = 1\) means they are equally
    severe.
  \item
    \(R_{\max}\) --- the \(R^2\) of the infeasible regression that
    included the unobservables.
  \end{itemize}
\item
  With \((\mathring\beta, \mathring R)\) from the short regression and
  \((\tilde\beta, \tilde R)\) from the controlled one, \[
  \beta^{*} \;\approx\; \tilde\beta \;-\; \delta\,\big(\mathring\beta - \tilde\beta\big)\,\frac{R_{\max} - \tilde R}{\tilde R - \mathring R}.
  \]
\item
  Two ways to report it: fix \(\delta = 1\) and give \(\beta^{*}\); or
  set \(\beta^{*} = 0\) and solve for the \(\delta\) that would be
  needed. Oster's calibration from randomized trials: \(\delta = 1\),
  \(R_{\max} = \min(1.3\,\tilde R,\, 1)\).
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Oster in practice: a warning}
\protect\phantomsection\label{oster-in-practice-a-warning}
Simulated data, one unobserved confounder, \textbf{true effect
\(= 1.000\)}:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\toprule
 & $\beta$ & $R^2$ \\
\midrule
short: $Y \sim D$ & $2.086$ & $0.452$ \\
controlled: $Y \sim D + X$ & $1.743$ & $0.484$ \\
infeasible: $Y \sim D + X + U$ & $1.014$ & $0.562$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Oster's default rule sets \(R_{\max} = 1.3 \times 0.484 = 0.629\), and
  returns \(\beta^{*} = \mathbf{0.201}\) --- a correction that
  overshoots the truth by a mile.
\item
  Feed it the \emph{true} \(R_{\max} = 0.562\) and it returns
  \(\beta^{*} = \mathbf{0.918}\), almost exactly right. The method
  works. The \textbf{guess} was what failed.
\item
  Meanwhile the \(\delta\) test passes: the \(\delta\) that would drive
  \(\beta^{*}\) to zero is \(1.13 > 1\).
\item
  \textbf{The lesson.} Report \(\delta\), which is a bounded comparison
  you can defend. Do not report \(\beta^{*}\) as if it were a point
  estimate --- it is a linear function of a number you guessed. If you
  show it, show it across a range of \(R_{\max}\).
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[fragile]{Cinelli and Hazlett (2020): the robustness value}
\protect\phantomsection\label{cinelli-and-hazlett-2020-the-robustness-value}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Same problem, third parameterization --- and the one that travels
  best, because it uses \textbf{partial \(R^2\)} rather than odds ratios
  or selection ratios.
\item
  \(RV_q\) is the minimum share of the residual variation that a
  confounder must explain in \textbf{both} \(D\) and \(Y\) in order to
  move the estimate by \(100q\%\).
\item
  In the regression above: \(t = 47.1\) and
  \(RV_{q=1} = \mathbf{0.48}\). Any confounder explaining less than
  \(48\%\) of what is left in both \(D\) and \(Y\) cannot drive the
  effect to zero.
\item
  Is that reassuring? Check it against the confounder we planted: \(U\)
  explains \(15\%\) of the residual variation in \(Y\) and \(34\%\) in
  \(D\). Below the bar --- and indeed it does not kill the effect, it
  moves it from \(1.743\) to \(1.014\).
\item
  The feature that sells it: \textbf{benchmark bounds.} Express \(RV\)
  as a multiple of an observed covariate's partial \(R^2\) --- ``the
  confounder would have to be \(3\times\) as important as firm size'\,'
  is a sentence a referee can evaluate.
\item
  Software: \texttt{sensemakr} (R and Stata), \texttt{psacalc} for
  Oster, \texttt{rbounds} and \texttt{mhbounds} for Rosenbaum.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\section{5. The NSW Demonstration}\label{the-nsw-demonstration}

\begin{frame}{The 1970s empirical crisis}
\protect\phantomsection\label{the-1970s-empirical-crisis}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  The methods in this lecture were not adopted because they were
  elegant. They were adopted after a crisis of confidence in
  observational work.
\item
  Job-training programs in the 1970s were evaluated observationally, and
  the estimates were all over the place --- depending, apparently, on
  the analyst.
\item
  The \textbf{National Supported Work (NSW) Demonstration} was
  different: it \emph{randomized} participation. So the experimental
  effect was known.
\item
  That created something rare and valuable: a \textbf{benchmark}. You
  could now ask whether observational methods, applied to the same
  treated units, recover the experimental answer.
\item
  Everything in this lecture gets graded against it.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What LaLonde did}
\protect\phantomsection\label{what-lalonde-did}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  LaLonde (1986) ran the decisive test. He took the NSW experiment,
  \textbf{threw away the experimental control group}, and replaced it
  with comparison groups built from survey data (CPS and PSID).
\item
  He then applied the standard observational toolkit of the day and
  compared the results to the experimental benchmark of roughly
  \textbf{\$1,800} in annual earnings gains.
\item
  The observational estimates ranged from a fraction of the benchmark to
  \textbf{the wrong sign} --- and which one you got depended heavily on
  how the comparison group was built.
\item
  The paper was devastating and hugely influential. It is a major reason
  the profession moved toward IV, RDD, and DiD --- the other river.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Dehejia and Wahba: not so fast}
\protect\phantomsection\label{dehejia-and-wahba-not-so-fast}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Dehejia and Wahba (1999, 2002)} revisited LaLonde's data with
  propensity score methods.
\item
  Their finding: the observational estimates \emph{did} recover the
  experimental benchmark reasonably well --- \textbf{once common support
  was imposed}.
\item
  The key diagnosis: much of LaLonde's failure was an \textbf{overlap}
  problem, not only a confounding problem. The CPS and PSID comparison
  groups contained enormous numbers of people utterly unlike the NSW
  participants.
\item
  Discard the non-comparable units and the methods perform far better.
\item
  This is why the distinction of Lecture 3a, §3, Identification
  Assumptions, matters so much. Two different assumptions were failing,
  and only one of them is checkable.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Smith and Todd: not so fast either}
\protect\phantomsection\label{smith-and-todd-not-so-fast-either}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Smith and Todd (2005)} re-examined the Dehejia--Wahba result
  and found it fragile.
\item
  The success was \textbf{sensitive to the subsample} analysed and to
  the \textbf{propensity score specification} chosen. Reasonable
  alternative choices undid it.
\item
  Their charge is uncomfortable and worth stating plainly: the
  specification that worked may have been found \emph{because} the
  experimental answer was already known.
\item
  You cannot run that search in real research. In a genuine application
  there is no benchmark to check against --- that is the entire point of
  doing the study.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The honest summary}
\protect\phantomsection\label{the-honest-summary}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Selection on observables \textbf{can} work --- when overlap is genuine
  and the confounders are truly measured.
\item
  Neither condition is verifiable in the setting where you need them:

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Unconfoundedness is \textbf{untestable}, always.
  \item
    Common support is \textbf{checkable}.
  \end{itemize}
\item
  So the practical lesson is asymmetric: do the check you can do, and
  argue explicitly for the assumption you cannot.
\item
  \textbf{A useful exercise}: the NSW data are freely available. Apply
  every method in this lecture and compare them to the \$1,800
  benchmark. Cunningham's chapter walks through it, and it is the
  fastest way to develop instincts about when these methods hold up.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\section{Wrapping Up}\label{wrapping-up}

\begin{frame}[fragile]{Summary {[}Part 1{]}: the score and what it does}
\protect\phantomsection\label{summary-part-1-the-score-and-what-it-does}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{The propensity score} reduces \(X\) to one number and defeats
  the curse of dimensionality for \emph{conditioning}. It does nothing
  for \textbf{identification} --- unconfoundedness is still assumed,
  never delivered.
\item
  \textbf{Judge the score by balance achieved, not by fit.} A high
  pseudo-\(R^2\) with bad balance is a bad score; the reverse is fine.
  The whole stage is blind to \(Y\), which is what makes the
  specification search legitimate.
\item
  \textbf{Balance means the distribution, not the mean.} Standardized
  differences \emph{and} variance ratios. Our \texttt{Volatility}
  covariate had \(\text{SMD} = -0.010\) and a variance ratio of
  \(2.27\).
\item
  \textbf{IPW} reweights the sample into a pseudo-experiment:
  \(1/\hat e\) for treated and \(1/(1-\hat e)\) for controls builds the
  population; \(\hat e/(1-\hat e)\) on controls alone rebuilds the
  \emph{treated} distribution. Watch the tails, and normalize.
\item
  \textbf{Propensity score matching} is the one use with a serious case
  against it: King and Nielsen (2019) show it targets complete
  randomization when you could have had blocking, so pruning can
  \emph{increase} imbalance.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Summary {[}Part 2{]}: the verdict}
\protect\phantomsection\label{summary-part-2-the-verdict}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Regression is a conditioning strategy too}, not a rival
  family. FWL shows it uses the residual variation in \(D\), which is
  why it weights strata by \(N_j p_j(1-p_j)\): \(2.167\) versus
  matching's \(2.265\) in our example.
\item
  \textbf{Matching creates no new exogenous variation.} If OLS was
  endogenous, matching is endogenous. It cannot fix an unobserved
  confounder, reverse causality, or a bad control.
\item
  \textbf{Sensitivity analysis is the honest response} to an untestable
  assumption. Report \(\Gamma^{*}\), or \(\delta\), or a robustness
  value --- and calibrate it against a covariate you actually measured.
\item
  \textbf{NSW}: these methods can work when overlap is real and the
  confounders are measured --- and you can only ever verify one of those
  two.
\item
  Next lecture: designs that \emph{create} variation instead of assuming
  it away.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{A checklist for your own paper}
\protect\phantomsection\label{a-checklist-for-your-own-paper}
\begin{enumerate}
\tightlist
\item
  State the \textbf{estimand} before the estimator. \(ATE\), \(ATT\), or
  \(ATU\) --- and say why the policy question needs that one.
\item
  Defend the \textbf{assignment mechanism} in institutional terms. Name
  the confounders and say how you measured them.
\item
  Justify every covariate as \textbf{pre-treatment}. Any post-treatment
  control needs an argument, not a footnote.
\item
  \textbf{Plot the overlap.} Report what you trimmed and how the
  estimand changed.
\item
  Report \textbf{balance}, not fit: standardized differences and
  variance ratios, before and after.
\item
  Report \textbf{matching and regression}. If they disagree, diagnose
  whether it is weights or extrapolation.
\item
  Run a \textbf{sensitivity analysis}, and calibrate the threshold
  against an observed covariate.
\item
  Run a \textbf{placebo} on a pre-treatment period.
\item
  Say plainly what would break the design --- and what unobservable
  would have to exist.
\end{enumerate}
\end{frame}
