\section{1. Propensity Scores}\label{propensity-scores}

\begin{frame}{Defining the propensity score}
\protect\phantomsection\label{defining-the-propensity-score}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Propensity score}: \(e(X) = \Pr(D = 1 \mid X)\)

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    ``the conditional probability of assignment to a particular
    treatment given a vector of observed covariates''
  \end{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{Balancing property} (Rosenbaum--Rubin 1983):
  \(X \perp D \mid \Pr(D = 1 \mid X)\)

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    \(E[X \mid D=1, \Pr(D=1 \mid X)] = E[X \mid D=0, \Pr(D=1 \mid X)]\)
  \item
    Covariate distributions are similar between treatment and control
    conditional the propensity score.
  \end{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{Propensity score theorem} (Rosenbaum--Rubin 1983): if
  \((Y^1,Y^0) \indep D \mid X\), then \[
  (Y^1, Y^0) \;\indep\; D \;\mid\; e(X)
  \]

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Conditioning on a \textbf{single number} does what conditioning on
    all of \(X\) does. Ten covariates collapse to one scalar --- the
    curse of dimensionality defeated, for \emph{conditioning}.
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Estimating the score}
\protect\phantomsection\label{estimating-the-score}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  You know the \textbf{true} score only when you ran the experiment
  yourself. Otherwise, it must be estimated:
  \(\Pr(D_i {=} 1 \mid X_i) = F(\beta X_i)\), fitted by \textbf{logit or
  probit}.
\item
  Judge the specification by \textbf{balance achieved}, not by fit. A
  score with a high pseudo-\(R^2\) but poor balance is a bad score; the
  reverse is fine.
\item
  Fit, check balance, add interactions and polynomials where balance
  fails, refit. A legitimate specification search, because it never
  looks at \(Y\) --- the whole stage is \textbf{blind to the outcome},
  which protects against fishing. Say so in the paper.
\item
  \textbf{The score creates no support that \(X\) lacks.} If a covariate
  cell is all-treated or all-control, collapsing \(X\) to a scalar hides
  that; it does not fix it.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Checking balance: two numbers, not a \(t\)-test}
\protect\phantomsection\label{checking-balance-two-numbers-not-a-t-test}
The balancing property is checkable. Report it covariate by covariate,
before and after.

\[
\text{SMD} \;=\; \frac{\bar X_1 - \bar X_0}{\sqrt{\big(s_1^2 + s_0^2\big)/2}},
\qquad\qquad
\text{VR} \;=\; \frac{s_1^2}{s_0^2}
\]

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Standardized mean difference.} \(|\text{SMD}| < 0.1\) is good.

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Keep the denominator \textbf{fixed at its raw-sample value} when
    recomputing after weighting, or the yardstick moves with the thing
    you are measuring.
  \end{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{Variance ratio.} Means can match while spreads do not. Rubin:
  stay inside \([0.5,\,2]\), ideally \([0.8,\,1.25]\).
\item
  \textbf{Do not use \(t\)-tests.} Balance is a property of \emph{this
  sample}, not a population hypothesis --- and \(p\) confounds imbalance
  with sample size (Imai, King and Stuart 2008).
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[fragile]{Reading the balance plot}
\protect\phantomsection\label{reading-the-balance-plot}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Left panel, the success.} Four covariates start badly
  imbalanced --- \texttt{Size} at \(0.65\), \texttt{Leverage} and
  \texttt{ROA} near \(0.49\). After weighting, every one is under
  \(0.02\). This is the plot referees want, and it is the one most
  papers stop at.
\item
  \textbf{Left panel, the trap.} \texttt{Volatility} looks
  \emph{perfect} from the start: \(\text{SMD} = -0.010\), and a
  \(t\)-test returns \(p = 0.72\). On the evidence of means, nothing to
  see.
\item
  \textbf{Right panel.} Its variance ratio is \(\mathbf{2.27}\) ---
  treated firms have more than twice the spread. Extreme volatility
  drives selection in \emph{both} tails.
\item
  \textbf{Weighting makes it worse}, \(2.27 \to \mathbf{2.83}\), because
  the fitted score never contained \(\texttt{vol}^2\). Add that one term
  and the ratio falls to \(\mathbf{1.03}\).
\item
  The lesson of this section: judge the score by \textbf{balance
  achieved, not by fit} --- and balance means the whole distribution.
  Check squares and interactions, not just levels.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Three things to do with the score}
\protect\phantomsection\label{three-things-to-do-with-the-score}
Once every unit has a \(\hat e(X_i)\), there are three standard uses.

\begin{enumerate}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Diagnose.} Plot \(\hat e\) separately for treated and control
  units. This is the common-support check, and it costs nothing.
\item
  \textbf{Weight.} Reweight the sample to build a pseudo-experiment ---
  inverse probability weighting.
\item
  \textbf{Match.} Pair treated to control units with similar scores,
  then difference --- propensity score matching.
\end{enumerate}
\end{frame}

\protect\phantomsection\label{visualizing-overlap}

Right panel: a treated mass at \(\hat e \approx 1\) with no controls,

\begin{frame}{This plot belongs in every such paper}
\protect\phantomsection\label{this-plot-belongs-in-every-such-paper}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Common support is verifiable, unlike unconfoundedness.
\item
  What to do when it looks like the right panel:

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    \textbf{Trim} observations with extreme scores, or impose a caliper.
    Drop units with \(\hat e \notin [0.1,\,0.9]\). A rule of thumb, but
    a defensible and widely accepted one (Crump, Hotz, Imbens and
    Mitnik, 2009).
  \item
    \textbf{Report the change in estimand.} After trimming you describe
    a subpopulation, not the original one. Say how many observations
    went and how the treated sample shifted.
  \item
    Consider retreating to the \(ATT\), which only needs overlap in one
    direction.
  \end{itemize}
\item
  What \emph{not} to do: report the estimate and stay silent. The
  regression will happily extrapolate into a region with no data and
  give you a standard error that reflects none of that.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Inverse probability weighting}
\protect\phantomsection\label{inverse-probability-weighting}
Create balanced pseudo-population by inverse probability weighting.
Write \(\hat e_i\) for \(\hat e(X_i)\) and \(\pi = \Pr(D{=}1)\).

\small

\[
\begin{aligned}
ATE \;&=\; E\!\left[Y_1 - Y_0\right] = E\!\left[\frac{D\,Y}{e(X)}\right] - E\!\left[\frac{(1-D)\,Y}{1 - e(X)}\right] \\
ATT \;&=\; E\!\left[Y_1 - Y_0 \middle| D = 1 \right] = \frac{1}{\pi}\,\left(E\!\left[DY\right] - E\!\left[\frac{e(X)(1-D)Y}{1 - e(X)}\right]\right) \\[1em]
\widehat{ATE}_{IPW} \;&=\; \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[\frac{D_i}{\hat e_i} - \frac{1 - D_i}{1 - \hat e_i}\right] Y_i \\
\widehat{ATT}_{IPW} \;&=\; \frac{1}{n_T}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[D_i - (1 - D_i)\,\frac{\hat e_i}{1 - \hat e_i}\right] Y_i
\end{aligned}
\] \normalsize

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Both are \textbf{Horvitz--Thompson (1952)} forms.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why \emph{that} weight for the ATE?}
\protect\phantomsection\label{why-that-weight-for-the-ate}
We prove \(E\!\left[\frac{D\,Y}{e(X)}\right] = E[Y^1]\). The \(E[Y^0]\)
half is symmetric.

\scriptsize

\[
\begin{aligned}
E\!\left[ \frac{D \cdot Y}{e(X)} \right]
  &= E\!\left[ \frac{D \cdot Y^1}{e(X)} \right]
     && \text{switching equation (consistency): } D \cdot Y = D \cdot Y^1 \\
  &= E_X\!\left[ E\!\left( \frac{D \cdot Y^1}{e(X)} \,\Big|\, X \right) \right]
     && \text{law of total expectation} \\
  &= E_X\!\left[ \frac{1}{e(X)} \cdot E\big(D \cdot Y^1 \mid X\big) \right]
     && e(X) \text{ is fixed given } X \\
  &= E_X\!\left[ \frac{1}{e(X)} \cdot E(D \mid X) \cdot E\big(Y^1 \mid X\big) \right]
     && \text{unconfoundedness (exchangeability): } D \indep Y^1 \mid X \\
  &= E_X\!\left[ \frac{1}{e(X)} \cdot e(X) \cdot E\big(Y^1 \mid X\big) \right]
     && \text{by definition, } E(D \mid X) = e(X) \\
  &= E_X\big[ E(Y^1 \mid X) \big] \;=\; E[Y^1]
     && \text{cancel; law of total expectation}
\end{aligned}
\]
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why \emph{that} weight for the ATT?}
\protect\phantomsection\label{why-that-weight-for-the-att}
\scriptsize

\(E[Y^1 \mid D{=}1] = E[Y \mid D{=}1] = \frac{E[DY]}{E[D]}\). \[
\begin{aligned}
E\big[Y^0 \mid D{=}1\big]
  &= E_X\big[\, E(Y^0 \mid X, D{=}1) \,\big|\, D{=}1 \,\big]
     && \text{law of total expectation, over } f(x \mid D{=}1) \\
  &= E_X\big[\, E(Y^0 \mid X, D{=}0) \,\big|\, D{=}1 \,\big]
     && \text{unconfoundedness --- swap the arm} \\
  &= E_X\!\left[\, E(Y^0 \mid X, D{=}0)\cdot\frac{e(X)}{\pi} \,\right]
     && \text{Bayes: } f(x \mid D{=}1) = e(x)f(x)/\pi \\
  &= E_X\!\left[\, \frac{E\big[(1-D)\,Y \mid X\big]}{1 - e(X)}\cdot\frac{e(X)}{\pi} \,\right]
     && E[(1{-}D)Y \mid X] = \big(1{-}e(X)\big)E[Y^0 \mid X, D{=}0] \\
  &= E\!\left[\, \frac{(1 - D)\,Y\,e(X)}{\big(1 - e(X)\big)\,\pi} \,\right]
     && \text{law of total expectation}
\end{aligned}
\] \normalsize

Line 3: it converts an average over the \emph{treated} covariate
distribution into one over the \emph{whole} population.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Ten banks, split by size}
\protect\phantomsection\label{ten-banks-split-by-size}
Lecture 1's capital injections, now with a covariate: bank
\textbf{size}.

\begin{center}
\scriptsize
\begin{tabular}{crcrrrr}
\toprule
Bank & Size & $D$ & $Y^0$ & $Y^1$ & $\tau$ & observed $Y$ \\
\midrule
$1$  & Small & $1$ & $10$ & $12$ & $2$ & $\mathbf{12}$ \\
$2$  & Small & $0$ & $8$  & $10$ & $2$ & $\mathbf{8}$  \\
$3$  & Small & $0$ & $9$  & $11$ & $2$ & $\mathbf{9}$  \\
$4$  & Small & $0$ & $11$ & $13$ & $2$ & $\mathbf{11}$ \\
$5$  & Small & $0$ & $12$ & $14$ & $2$ & $\mathbf{12}$ \\
\midrule
$6$  & Large & $1$ & $18$ & $24$ & $6$ & $\mathbf{24}$ \\
$7$  & Large & $1$ & $19$ & $25$ & $6$ & $\mathbf{25}$ \\
$8$  & Large & $1$ & $21$ & $27$ & $6$ & $\mathbf{27}$ \\
$9$  & Large & $1$ & $22$ & $28$ & $6$ & $\mathbf{28}$ \\
$10$ & Large & $0$ & $20$ & $26$ & $6$ & $\mathbf{20}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Small banks are \textbf{rarely} rescued, \(1\) of \(5\); large banks
  \textbf{usually} are, \(4\) of \(5\). So \(e(\text{Small}) = 0.2\) and
  \(e(\text{Large}) = 0.8\).
\item
  Small banks gain \(2\), large banks gain \(6\). Truth: \(ATE = 4\),
  \(ATT = 5.2\).
\item
  Only the \textbf{bold} column is observed. Comparing rescued with not
  gives \(\mathbf{11.2}\) --- nearly three times the \(ATE\).
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The ATE rebuilds the population}
\protect\phantomsection\label{the-ate-rebuilds-the-population}
\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lrrrrrr}
\toprule
Cell & $N$ & $e$ & injected & $\times\,1/e$ & controls & $\times\,1/(1-e)$ \\
\midrule
Small & $5$ & $0.2$ & $1$ & $\times 5 = \mathbf{5}$    & $4$ & $\times 1.25 = \mathbf{5}$ \\
Large & $5$ & $0.8$ & $4$ & $\times 1.25 = \mathbf{5}$ & $1$ & $\times 5 = \mathbf{5}$ \\
\midrule
Total & $10$ & & $5$ & $\mathbf{10}$ & $5$ & $\mathbf{10}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Bank \(1\) is the \textbf{only} small bank rescued, so it must stand
  in for all five. Its weight is \(1/0.2 = 5\).
\item
  Every weighted entry comes back to \(5\): each arm is blown back up to
  the \textbf{full population of ten}. That is the
  \emph{pseudo-population}, and in it \(D \indep X\) by construction.
\item
  Before weighting, small banks are \(20\%\) of the injected sample and
  \(80\%\) of the controls. After weighting, \(50\%\) of both --- the
  imbalance that caused the bias is gone.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The ATT reweights controls only}
\protect\phantomsection\label{the-att-reweights-controls-only}
The \(ATT\) wants the \textbf{treated} distribution. The four large and
one small rescued banks are already a sample from it, so they keep
weight \(1\). Only the controls move.

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lrrrr}
\toprule
Cell & controls & $\times\ e/(1-e)$ & $=$ & injected \\
\midrule
Small & $4$ & $0.25$ & $\mathbf{1}$ & $1$ \\
Large & $1$ & $4.00$ & $\mathbf{4}$ & $4$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Multiplying the controls by \(e/(1-e)\) makes their count match the
  injected count \textbf{exactly} --- last column.
\item
  Bank \(10\) is the lone large control, and it must cover \textbf{four}
  rescued banks, so it carries weight \(4\). Push \(e\) toward \(1\) and
  there is nobody left to carry it: \textbf{this is where the \(ATT\)
  fails}, and why it needs only \(e(X) < 1\) while the \(ATE\) needs
  \(0 < e(X) < 1\).
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Does it work?}
\protect\phantomsection\label{does-it-work}
\[
\begin{aligned}
\widehat{E[Y^1]} &= \tfrac{1}{10}\!\left[\tfrac{12}{0.2} + \tfrac{24+25+27+28}{0.8}\right] = \tfrac{60 + 130}{10} = 19 \\
\widehat{E[Y^0]} &= \tfrac{1}{10}\!\left[\tfrac{8+9+11+12}{0.8} + \tfrac{20}{0.2}\right] = \tfrac{50 + 100}{10} = 15 \\[0.2em]
\widehat{ATT}\; &= \underbrace{\tfrac{12 + 24+25+27+28}{5}}_{\text{injected mean}\;=\;23.2} \;-\; \underbrace{\tfrac{(8+9+11+12)(0.25) + 20(4)}{4(0.25) + 1(4)}}_{\text{controls}\,\times\,\text{odds}\;=\;18.0}
\end{aligned}
\]

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Truth: \(ATE = 4\), \(ATT = 5.2\). The naive \(SDO\) is
  \(23.2 - 12 = \mathbf{11.2}\).
\item
  IPW returns \(19 - 15 = \mathbf{4}\) and
  \(23.2 - 18.0 = \mathbf{5.2}\). Both exactly right.
\item
  \(ATT > ATE\) because large banks gain more, and large banks are where
  the injections went. \textbf{Selection on gains --- the two estimands
  correctly disagree.}
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[fragile]{Two ways to divide}
\protect\phantomsection\label{two-ways-to-divide}
Both estimators use the same weights \(1/\hat e_i\). They differ
\textbf{only in the denominator}.

\vspace{-0.2em}

\[
\underbrace{\widehat{E[Y^1]}_{\text{HT}} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_i \frac{D_i Y_i}{\hat e_i}}_{\text{divide by } N}
\qquad\qquad
\underbrace{\widehat{E[Y^1]}_{\text{Hájek}} = \frac{1}{\sum_i D_i/\hat e_i}\sum_i \frac{D_i Y_i}{\hat e_i}}_{\text{divide by the weights you got}}
\]

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  In the \textbf{population} they agree: \(E[D/e(X)] = 1\), so the
  weights sum to \(N\).
\item
  In a \textbf{sample} they do not. \(\sum_i D_i/\hat e_i\) is only an
  \emph{estimate} of \(N\), and it misses.
\item
  Hájek's weights sum to one by construction, so it is always a genuine
  \textbf{weighted average of outcomes you actually observed}. HT
  carries no such guarantee.
\item
  The default in Stata's \texttt{teffects\ ipw} and R's
  \texttt{WeightIt}.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Example 1}
\protect\phantomsection\label{example-1}
Four rescued banks. \textbf{Every one has lending growth \(Y = 10\)}, so
the answer must be \(10\) for \(\widehat{E[Y^1]}\). Here \(N = 8\).

\begin{center}

\midrule

B & $0.50$ & $2$ & $10$ \\

\end{itemize}

\begin{frame}{Propensity score matching}
\protect\phantomsection\label{propensity-score-matching}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Match each treated unit to controls with a \textbf{similar score},
  then difference.
\item
  \textbf{Caliper} matching: refuse any match farther than some
  tolerance (say \(0.001\) on the score), \emph{discarding} the treated
  unit if none qualifies. \textbf{Radius} matching: average all controls
  inside the tolerance.
\item
  Two problems with both:

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    The tolerance is \textbf{chosen by the researcher} (Huntington-Klein
    et al.~2021).
  \item
    Dropping treated units \textbf{changes the estimand} --- the \(ATT\)
    summarised the \emph{original} treated sample.
  \end{itemize}
\item
  King \& Nielsen, 2019: Two units can share an identical score
  \(e(X_i) = e(X_j)\) despite having completely opposite covariate
  profiles \(X_i \neq X_j\). Matching purely on the scalar score can
  actually increase local covariate imbalance compared to multivariate
  distance matching (e.g., Coarsened Exact Matching).
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What the score does and does not buy}
\protect\phantomsection\label{what-the-score-does-and-does-not-buy}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  It buys \textbf{dimension reduction}, not identification. If \(X\)
  omits a confounder, \(e(X)\) omits it too.
\item
  Standard regression silently hides lack of common support through
  linear extrapolation, whereas plotting \(\hat{e}(X)\) immediately
  exposes severe positivity violations.
\item
  A high pseudo-\(R^2\) in a treatment model means covariates perfectly
  predict treatment assignment (\(e(X) \to 0\) or \(1\)). This breaks
  the positivity assumption and causes extreme IPW variance. Conversely,
  a low pseudo-\(R^2\) indicates that treatment assignment is nearly
  independent of \(X\) (resembling a randomized control trial).
\item
  IPW shifts the modeling assumption from \(E[Y \mid D, X]\) (outcome
  equation) to \(P(D = 1 \mid X)\) (treatment assignment equation).
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\section{2. Regression}\label{regression}

\begin{frame}{Crossing to the other side of the map}
\protect\phantomsection\label{crossing-to-the-other-side-of-the-map}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Everything so far has been an \textbf{interpolation} method:
  subclassification, matching, the propensity score. Each one refuses to
  compare a treated unit that has no counterpart. Common support is the
  binding constraint.
\item
  Regression sits on the other side. It does not need common support at
  all, and so \textbf{the curse of dimensionality simply does not
  arise}.
\item
  It buys that with a \textbf{functional form}. Where the data are
  silent, the fitted model speaks anyway: OLS imputes the missing
  counterfactual by \textbf{extrapolating} the outcome model into
  regions with no comparable units.
\item
  That is the trade. Matching needs overlap; regression needs a
  correctly specified outcome model.
\item
  Both still need unconfoundedness. Nothing in this section relaxes it.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Matching vs.~regression}
\protect\phantomsection\label{matching-vs.-regression}
\pandocbounded{\includegraphics[keepaspectratio]{03b_propensity_scores_files/figure-beamer/unnamed-chunk-5-1.pdf}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The bivariate case is easy; the multivariate case looks
hard}
\protect\phantomsection\label{the-bivariate-case-is-easy-the-multivariate-case-looks-hard}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  With one regressor, \[
  Y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X_i + u_i,
  \qquad
  \hat\beta_1 = \frac{\mathrm{Cov}(Y_i, X_i)}{\mathrm{Var}(X_i)} .
  \]
\item
  The coefficient is the slope of the best-fit line, a
  \textbf{covariance scaled by a variance}.
\item
  Now add covariates: \[
  Y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X_{1i} + \beta_2 X_{2i} + \cdots + \beta_k X_{ki} + \varepsilon_i .
  \]
\item
  In a multivariate regression the coefficients are \textbf{not} simple
  scaled covariances. But the Frisch--Waugh--Lovell theorem (Frisch and
  Waugh 1933; Lovell 1963) says they are scaled covariances of something
  else.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Frisch--Waugh--Lovell in three steps}
\protect\phantomsection\label{frischwaughlovell-in-three-steps}
\textbf{Step 1. Auxiliary regression}: \[
X_{1i} = \gamma_0 + \gamma_2 X_{2i} + \cdots + \gamma_K X_{Ki} + f_i .
\] \(\hat X_{1i}\) is the part of \(X_{1i}\) the other covariates
explain.

\textbf{Step 2. Residualization}: \[
\tilde X_{1i} = X_{1i} - \hat X_{1i} .
\] The variation in \(X_{1i}\) that is \emph{uncorrelated} with
everything else --- \textbf{partialling out}.

\textbf{Step 3. Regress the outcome on the residual.} \[
Y_i = \alpha + \beta_1 \tilde X_{1i} + \varepsilon_i,
\qquad
\hat\beta_1 = \frac{\mathrm{Cov}(Y_i, \tilde X_{1i})}{\mathrm{Var}(\tilde X_{1i})} .
\]

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \(\hat\beta_1\) is \textbf{identical} to the coefficient on \(X_{1i}\)
  in the full regression.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{FWL is an identity, not an approximation}
\protect\phantomsection\label{fwl-is-an-identity-not-an-approximation}
Simulation: \(4{,}000\) workers, earnings on a treatment indicator
\(d\), age, and high-school GPA, with a true effect of \(\$1{,}500\).

\begin{center}
\footnotesize
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\toprule
 & (1) Multivariate OLS & (2) FWL \\
\midrule
$d$   & $1503.201$ & $1503.201$ \\
      & $(8.249)$  & $(8.287)$ \\
age   & $1.173$    & --- \\
      & $(2.023)$  & \\
gpa   & $9.081$    & --- \\
      & $(4.615)$  & \\
$N$   & $4000$     & $4000$ \\
\bottomrule

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  The estimate sits \(0.39\) above the true \(1500\) --- sampling noise.
\item
  The standard errors do \emph{not} agree, and that is not sampling

\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Residualize both sides}
\protect\phantomsection\label{residualize-both-sides}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Another version residualizes the outcome too:
  \begin{align}
  \tilde Y_i \;=\; Y_i \;-\; \hat Y_i &\quad\text{from}\quad Y_i = \lambda_0 + \lambda_2 X_{2i} + \cdots + \lambda_K X_{Ki} + v_i,\\
  &\quad\text{then}\quad \tilde Y_i = \beta_1 \tilde X_{1i} + \varepsilon_i.
  \end{align}
\item
  Both versions return \textbf{exactly the same \(\hat\beta_1\)}.
\item
  But the double-residual version buys one more thing: \textbf{its
  residuals are the residuals of the full regression}.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Three regressions, one coefficient}
\protect\phantomsection\label{three-regressions-one-coefficient}
\begin{center}

\begin{itemize}

\item
  Residualize only \(D\): the residuals still carry everything age and

\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why FWL is worth knowing}
\protect\phantomsection\label{why-fwl-is-worth-knowing}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{It tells you what variation identifies your coefficient.}
  \(\hat\beta_1\) uses only the part of \(X_1\) that the controls cannot
  explain.
\item
  \textbf{It makes the estimate visualizable.} Residualize \emph{both}
  sides against the controls and scatter \(\tilde Y\) against
  \(\tilde X_1\): a multivariate coefficient becomes a two-dimensional
  picture whose slope is
  \(\hat\beta_1\).\footnote{Filoso (2013) gives the worked proof and a Stata implementation.}
\item
  \textbf{It is the engine behind a great deal else.} Fixed effects,
  double machine learning, and the weighting results later in this
  section are all FWL in disguise.
\item
  Angrist and Pischke (2009) call this \textbf{regression anatomy}, and
  it is the single most useful algebraic fact about OLS.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Exogeneity, and the canonical OLS model}
\protect\phantomsection\label{exogeneity-and-the-canonical-ols-model}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Write the \textbf{canonical OLS model}: \[
  Y_i = \alpha + \delta D_i + \gamma X_i + \varepsilon_i .
  \]
\item
  For \(\hat\delta\) to be causal, \(D_i\) must be \emph{exogenous}
  \(\Rightarrow\) Imbens and Rubin (2015) put it plainly: exogeneity
  ``combines unconfoundedness with functional form and constant
  treatment effect assumptions that are quite strong, and arguably
  unnecessary.''
\item
  Three assumptions, not one. Unconfoundedness you have already met and
  argued for. The other two arrived silently, in the act of writing down
  the equation.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Taking conditional expectations}
\protect\phantomsection\label{taking-conditional-expectations}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Take expectations of the canonical model separately for the treated
  and the control group: \[
  \begin{aligned}
  E[Y_i \mid D_i = 1, X_i] &= \alpha + \delta\, E[D_i \mid D_i = 1, X_i] + \gamma_{1} E[X_i \mid D_i = 1, X_i] \\
  E[Y_i \mid D_i = 0, X_i] &= \alpha + \delta\, E[D_i \mid D_i = 0, X_i] + \gamma_{1} E[X_i \mid D_i = 0, X_i]
  \end{aligned}
  \]
\item
  Now replace \(Y\) with \(Y^1\) or \(Y^0\) as appropriate --- and do
  the same for \(X\).
\item
  Why would \(X\) have potential outcomes? \textbf{If \(X^1 \neq X^0\),
  then \(D\) causes \(X\), and \(X\) is an outcome, not a covariate.}
  That is the bad-control problem.
\item
  And allow the covariate to matter differently in the two worlds,
  \(\gamma_{11}\) against \(\gamma_{01}\): \[
  \begin{aligned}
  E[Y^1_i \mid D_i = 1, X_i] &= \alpha + \delta\, E[D_i \mid D_i = 1, X^1_i] + \gamma_{11} E[X^1_i \mid D_i = 1, X^1_i] \\
  E[Y^0_i \mid D_i = 0, X_i] &= \alpha + \delta\, E[D_i \mid D_i = 0, X^0_i] + \gamma_{01} E[X^0_i \mid D_i = 0, X^0_i]
  \end{aligned}
  \]
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What \(\gamma_{11} \neq \gamma_{01}\) actually means}
\protect\phantomsection\label{what-gamma_11-neq-gamma_01-actually-means}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Let \(Y^1\) be earnings with a college degree, \(Y^0\) earnings
  without one, and \(X\) be age.
\item
  Then \(\gamma_{11}\) is the age--earnings profile for \textbf{college
  graduates} and \(\gamma_{01}\) is the age--earnings profile for the
  same people \textbf{had they not gone}.
\item
  \(\gamma_{11} \neq \gamma_{01}\) \textbf{is} heterogeneous treatment
  effects --- the return to treatment varying with \(X\).
\item
  In finance the same statement is unremarkable: the effect of an index
  inclusion, a rating change, or a governance shock varies with firm
  size. If it does, \(\gamma_{11} \neq \gamma_{01}\).
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Heterogeneous treatment effect bias}
\protect\phantomsection\label{heterogeneous-treatment-effect-bias}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  OLS on the canonical model is a difference in conditional means: \[
  \hat\delta_{OLS} = E[Y^1_i \mid D_i = 1, X^1_i] - E[Y^0_i \mid D_i = 0, X^0_i].
  \]
\item
  Substitute the two expressions from the previous slide. \[
  \hat\delta_{OLS} \;=\; \delta \;+\; \underbrace{\Big(\gamma_{11} E[X^1_i \mid D_i = 1, X^1_i] \;-\; \gamma_{01} E[X^0_i \mid D_i = 0, X^0_i]\Big)}_{\text{heterogeneous treatment effect bias}}
  \]
\item
  This is what the canonical OLS model identifies. Not \(\delta\).
  \(\delta\) plus a term that has nothing to do with omitted variables.
\item
  Notice what is \emph{not} in the bias term: any unobserved confounder.
  We assumed unconfoundedness holds perfectly and there is nothing left
  to omit.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The two assumptions you signed up for}
\protect\phantomsection\label{the-two-assumptions-you-signed-up-for}
The bias term vanishes if and only if \textbf{both} of the following
hold.

\begin{enumerate}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Covariates are not outcomes.} \(X^1 = X^0 = X\): the
  covariates do not respond to treatment.
\item
  \textbf{Treatment effects are homogeneous in \(X\).}
  \(\gamma_{11} = \gamma_{01} = \gamma_1\): the return to treatment does
  not vary with the covariates.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Under A1 and A2, \(\hat\delta_{OLS} = \delta\). Fail either one and
  the canonical model is biased.
\item
  \textbf{A1 has no repair.} Do not control for a collider or a mediator
  \(\Rightarrow\) Lecture 2!
\item
  \textbf{A2 does have a repair}, and it is a change to the
  specification, not to the data. That is the next slide.
\item
  Note how strong A2 is. Neither matching nor the propensity score ever
  required it: they average heterogeneous effects, they do not assume
  them away.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Regression adjustment: Oaxaca's answer}
\protect\phantomsection\label{regression-adjustment-oaxacas-answer}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  The problem was never OLS. The problem was that \textbf{one
  specification} --- additive and separable.
\item
  The \textbf{Kitagawa--Oaxaca--Blinder decomposition} splits a mean gap
  into a part attributable to differences in \emph{endowments}
  (education, experience) and a part attributable to differences in the
  \emph{returns} to those endowments.
\item
  Słoczyński (2015, 2020) showed the decomposition is also a
  \textbf{treatment effects estimator} --- and that it identifies both
  the \(ATE\) and the \(ATT\). This is what ``regression adjustment''
  means.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Deriving the specification}
\protect\phantomsection\label{deriving-the-specification}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Write both potential outcomes, letting \(X\) act differently on each:
  \[
  \begin{aligned}
  Y^0_i &= \alpha + \gamma_{01} X_i + \varepsilon_i \\
  Y^1_i &= \alpha + \gamma_{01} X_i + \delta D_i + \gamma_{11} X_i \cdot D_i + \varepsilon_i
  \end{aligned}
  \]
\item
  Take conditional expectations, then form
  \(\delta = E[Y^1_i \mid D_i, X_i] - E[Y^0_i \mid D_i, X_i]\): \small
  \[
  \begin{aligned}
  &= \big(\alpha + \gamma_{01} E[X_i \mid D_i{=}0, X_i] + \delta + \gamma_{11} E[X_i \cdot D_i \mid D_i{=}1, X_i]\big) - \big(\alpha + \gamma_{01} E[X_i \mid D_i{=}0, X_i]\big) \\[0.2em]
  &= \delta + \gamma_{11} E[X_i \cdot D_i \mid D_i = 1]
  \end{aligned}
  \] \normalsize
\item
  The \(\gamma_{01}\) terms cancel because the \emph{same} coefficient
  appears in both potential outcomes. The interaction is what remains
  --- and it is exactly the term the canonical model omitted.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The regression adjustment estimator}
\protect\phantomsection\label{the-regression-adjustment-estimator}
To identify the \(ATE\) and the \(ATT\) under unconfoundedness
\textbf{with heterogeneous effects}, run \[
Y_i = \alpha + \delta D_i + \gamma_{01} X_i + \gamma_{11}\, D_i \cdot X_i + \varepsilon_i .
\]

\begin{itemize}
\item
  Interact the treatment with \textbf{every} covariate. Then \[
  \widehat{ATE} = \hat\delta, \qquad \widehat{ATT} = \hat\delta + \hat\gamma_{11}\, E[X_i \mid D_i = 1],
  \] where \(E[X_i \mid D_i = 1]\) is the covariate mean \textbf{in the
  treated group
  only}.\footnote{The $ATE$ result requires $X$ to be demeaned in the full sample. If you interact with terms that are not mean-zero --- squares, cross-products --- add them back: $\widehat{ATE} = \hat\delta + \sum_k \hat\gamma_{1k}\bar X_k$.}
\item
  The difference between the two estimands is now visible and
  mechanical: the same coefficients, evaluated at a different covariate
  distribution.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Does it work?}
\protect\phantomsection\label{does-it-work-1}
Simulation: \(5{,}000\) workers, \(5{,}000\) with a college degree; two
exogenous covariates, age and GPA, with \textbf{different means in the
treated and control groups}; both recentred, plus their squares and
their interaction. Then \[
\begin{aligned}
Y^0 &= 15000 + 10.25\,\text{age} - 10.5\,\text{age}^2 + 1000\,\text{gpa} - 10.5\,\text{gpa}^2 + 500\,(\text{age}\cdot\text{gpa}) + e \\
Y^1 &= Y^0 + 2500 + 100\,\text{age} + 1100\,\text{gpa}
\end{aligned}
\]

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Because age and GPA are recentred, \(E[Y^1 - Y^0] = \$2{,}500\)
  exactly. The \(ATT\) is \textbf{not} \(\$2{,}500\): the treated group
  has a different age and GPA composition, and the effect varies with
  both.
\item
  The canonical model here controls for age, age\(^2\), GPA, GPA\(^2\)
  and their interaction. Every covariate needed for unconfoundedness is
  in it. Nothing is omitted.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{When is the canonical model actually wrong?}
\protect\phantomsection\label{when-is-the-canonical-model-actually-wrong}
Simulation, varying the \textbf{share treated}:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lccccc}
\toprule
Share treated & $0.20$ & $0.35$ & $0.50$ & $0.65$ & $0.80$ \\
\midrule
True $ATE$          & $2500$ & $2500$ & $2500$ & $2500$ & $2500$ \\
True $ATT$          & $1813$ & $1942$ & $2070$ & $2199$ & $2328$ \\
Canonical OLS       & $2097$ & $2310$ & $2500$ & $2689$ & $2902$ \\
Regression adj.\ ($ATE$) & $2500$ & $2500$ & $2499$ & $2499$ & $2499$ \\
Regression adj.\ ($ATT$) & $1812$ & $1941$ & $2069$ & $2198$ & $2327$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Regression adjustment recovers \textbf{both} estimands at every split.
\item
  At exactly \(50/50\) it lands on the \(ATE\) --- \textbf{by
  coincidence of the design, not by validity}.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What regression adjustment does not buy}
\protect\phantomsection\label{what-regression-adjustment-does-not-buy}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Regression adjustment removes the \textbf{constant-effects}
  assumption. It does not remove the \textbf{functional form}
  assumption, and cannot.
\item
  Inside the model is a model of the missing potential outcome. In the
  simulation the true \(Y^0\) contained age, age\(^2\), GPA, GPA\(^2\)
  and age\(\,\cdot\,\)GPA --- so the treatment had to be interacted with
  \textbf{all five}.
\item
  Drop just one interaction and it breaks. With the treatment effect
  also varying in age\(\,\cdot\,\)GPA, the fully interacted model
  returns \(2576\) against a true \(ATE\) of \(2575\); omitting the
  single \(D \cdot (\text{age}\cdot\text{gpa})\) term returns \(2368\).
\item
  \textbf{The functional-form requirement replaces the common-support
  requirement. It is not free.} You have swapped a checkable condition
  for an uncheckable one.
\item
  This is the honest summary of the trade made at the start of the
  section.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Three things wrong with the canonical model}
\protect\phantomsection\label{three-things-wrong-with-the-canonical-model}
\begin{enumerate}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{It assumes constant treatment effects.} Neither matching nor
  the propensity score ever asked for this. Regression adjustment
  removes it, at the cost of interacting the treatment with everything.
\item
  \textbf{Its weights are wrong.} Słoczyński (2022) shows the
  coefficient is \(w_1 \cdot ATT + w_0 \cdot ATU\), with each group's
  weight \textbf{inversely} proportional to its size; \textbf{it is
  generally none of the three}.
\item
  \textbf{It can only ever deliver one number.} There is one treatment
  indicator, so there is one coefficient. Regression adjustment delivers
  the \(ATE\) and the \(ATT\) from the same fit.
\end{enumerate}
\end{frame}

\section{3. What Matching Cannot Do}\label{what-matching-cannot-do}

\begin{frame}{Matching creates no new variation}
\protect\phantomsection\label{matching-creates-no-new-variation}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Matching \textbf{does not introduce exogenous variation}. It
  reorganizes and reweights the data you already have, using observables
  you already measured.
\item
  Consequence: if your OLS regression was biased by endogeneity,
  \textbf{the matched estimate is biased too}.
\item
  Contrast this with the designs still to come:

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    \textbf{IV} imports variation from outside the model.
  \item
    \textbf{RDD} exploits assignment that is locally as good as random.
  \item
    \textbf{DiD} differences out fixed unobservables.
  \end{itemize}
\item
  Matching belongs to a different category. It is a \emph{conditioning
  strategy}, like regression --- not a research design.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What it cannot fix}
\protect\phantomsection\label{what-it-cannot-fix}
Matching is powerless against:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Unobserved confounders.} You cannot match on what you cannot
  measure.
\item
  \textbf{Reverse causality.} Matching on \(X\) says nothing about
  whether \(Y\) caused \(D\).
\item
  \textbf{Measurement error} in treatment or outcome.
\item
  \textbf{Bad controls.} Matching on a post-treatment variable is
  exactly as damaging as controlling for it --- Lecture 2 applies
  unchanged, and the collider \(F\) of Lecture 3a, §2, Confounders,
  Covariates, and Colliders, is the warning.
\end{itemize}

\vspace{0.4em}

\begin{center}
\emph{You cannot match on what you cannot measure.}
\end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{So what is it good for?}
\protect\phantomsection\label{so-what-is-it-good-for}
Given all that, matching still earns its place:

\begin{enumerate}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{A non-parametric benchmark.} It estimates the effect without
  assuming a functional form. If matching and OLS disagree sharply, your
  specification is doing more work than you thought.
\item
  \textbf{Common-support discipline.} Matching refuses to compare units
  with no counterpart. Regression silently extrapolates into regions
  with no data.
\item
  \textbf{Diagnostic value.} The propensity score distribution reveals
  overlap problems that a regression table hides completely.
\item
  \textbf{Robustness when the functional form is wrong.} Matching can
  outperform a misspecified regression in finite samples.
\end{enumerate}

\vspace{0.3em}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Use it as a \emph{complement} to regression and a check on it --- not
  as an identification strategy.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\section{4. The NSW Demonstration}\label{the-nsw-demonstration}

\begin{frame}{The 1970s empirical crisis}
\protect\phantomsection\label{the-1970s-empirical-crisis}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  The methods in this lecture were not adopted because they were
  elegant. They were adopted after a crisis of confidence in
  observational work.
\item
  Job-training programs in the 1970s were evaluated observationally, and
  the estimates were all over the place --- depending, apparently, on
  the analyst.
\item
  The \textbf{National Supported Work (NSW) Demonstration} was
  different: it \emph{randomized} participation. So the experimental
  effect was known.
\item
  That created something rare and valuable: a \textbf{benchmark}. You
  could now ask whether observational methods, applied to the same
  treated units, recover the experimental answer.
\item
  Everything in this lecture gets graded against it.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What LaLonde did}
\protect\phantomsection\label{what-lalonde-did}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  LaLonde (1986) ran the decisive test. He took the NSW experiment,
  \textbf{threw away the experimental control group}, and replaced it
  with comparison groups built from survey data (CPS and PSID).
\item
  He then applied the standard observational toolkit of the day and
  compared the results to the experimental benchmark of roughly
  \textbf{\$1,800} in annual earnings gains.
\item
  The observational estimates ranged from a fraction of the benchmark to
  \textbf{the wrong sign} --- and which one you got depended heavily on
  how the comparison group was built.
\item
  The paper was devastating and hugely influential. It is a major reason
  the profession moved toward IV, RDD, and DiD --- the other river.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Dehejia and Wahba: not so fast}
\protect\phantomsection\label{dehejia-and-wahba-not-so-fast}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Dehejia and Wahba (1999, 2002)} revisited LaLonde's data with
  propensity score methods.
\item
  Their finding: the observational estimates \emph{did} recover the
  experimental benchmark reasonably well --- \textbf{once common support
  was imposed}.
\item
  The key diagnosis: much of LaLonde's failure was an \textbf{overlap}
  problem, not only a confounding problem. The CPS and PSID comparison
  groups contained enormous numbers of people utterly unlike the NSW
  participants.
\item
  Discard the non-comparable units and the methods perform far better.
\item
  This is why the distinction of Lecture 3a, §3, Identification
  Assumptions, matters so much. Two different assumptions were failing,
  and only one of them is checkable.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Smith and Todd: not so fast either}
\protect\phantomsection\label{smith-and-todd-not-so-fast-either}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Smith and Todd (2005)} re-examined the Dehejia--Wahba result
  and found it fragile.
\item
  The success was \textbf{sensitive to the subsample} analysed and to
  the \textbf{propensity score specification} chosen. Reasonable
  alternative choices undid it.
\item
  Their charge is uncomfortable and worth stating plainly: the
  specification that worked may have been found \emph{because} the
  experimental answer was already known.
\item
  You cannot run that search in real research. In a genuine application
  there is no benchmark to check against --- that is the entire point of
  doing the study.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The honest summary}
\protect\phantomsection\label{the-honest-summary}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Selection on observables \textbf{can} work --- when overlap is genuine
  and the confounders are truly measured.
\item
  Neither condition is verifiable in the setting where you need them:

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Unconfoundedness is \textbf{untestable}, always.
  \item
    Common support is \textbf{checkable}.
  \end{itemize}
\item
  So the practical lesson is asymmetric: do the check you can do, and
  argue explicitly for the assumption you cannot.
\item
  \textbf{A useful exercise}: the NSW data are freely available. Apply
  every method in this lecture and compare them to the \$1,800
  benchmark. Cunningham's chapter walks through it, and it is the
  fastest way to develop instincts about when these methods hold up.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\section{Wrapping Up}\label{wrapping-up}

\begin{frame}[fragile]{Summary {[}Part 1{]}: the score and what it does}
\protect\phantomsection\label{summary-part-1-the-score-and-what-it-does}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{The propensity score} reduces \(X\) to one number and defeats
  the curse of dimensionality for \emph{conditioning}. It does nothing
  for \textbf{identification} --- unconfoundedness is still assumed,
  never delivered.
\item
  \textbf{Judge the score by balance achieved, not by fit.} A high
  pseudo-\(R^2\) with bad balance is a bad score; the reverse is fine.
  The whole stage is blind to \(Y\), which is what makes the
  specification search legitimate.
\item
  \textbf{Balance means the distribution, not the mean.} Standardized
  differences \emph{and} variance ratios. Our \texttt{Volatility}
  covariate had \(\text{SMD} = -0.010\) and a variance ratio of
  \(2.27\).
\item
  \textbf{IPW} reweights the sample into a pseudo-experiment:
  \(1/\hat e\) for treated and \(1/(1-\hat e)\) for controls builds the
  population; \(\hat e/(1-\hat e)\) on controls alone rebuilds the
  \emph{treated} distribution. Watch the tails, and normalize.
\item
  \textbf{Propensity score matching} is the one use with a serious case
  against it: King and Nielsen (2019) show it targets complete
  randomization when you could have had blocking, so pruning can
  \emph{increase} imbalance.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Summary {[}Part 2{]}: the verdict}
\protect\phantomsection\label{summary-part-2-the-verdict}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Regression is a conditioning strategy too}, not a rival
  family. FWL shows it uses only the \textbf{residual variation in
  \(D\)} --- and Słoczyński's weights show that the coefficient this
  produces is generally neither the \(ATE\) nor the \(ATT\).
\item
  \textbf{Matching creates no new exogenous variation.} If OLS was
  endogenous, matching is endogenous. It cannot fix an unobserved
  confounder, reverse causality, or a bad control.
\item
  \textbf{NSW}: these methods can work when overlap is real and the
  confounders are measured --- and you can only ever verify one of those
  two.
\item
  Next lecture: designs that \emph{create} variation instead of assuming
  it away.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{A checklist for your own paper}
\protect\phantomsection\label{a-checklist-for-your-own-paper}
\begin{enumerate}
\tightlist
\item
  State the \textbf{estimand} before the estimator. \(ATE\), \(ATT\), or
  \(ATU\) --- and say why the policy question needs that one.
\item
  Defend the \textbf{assignment mechanism} in institutional terms. Name
  the confounders and say how you measured them.
\item
  Justify every covariate as \textbf{pre-treatment}. Any post-treatment
  control needs an argument, not a footnote.
\item
  \textbf{Plot the overlap.} Report what you trimmed and how the
  estimand changed.
\item
  Report \textbf{balance}, not fit: standardized differences and
  variance ratios, before and after.
\item
  Report \textbf{matching and regression}. If they disagree, diagnose
  whether it is weights or extrapolation.
\item
  Run a \textbf{sensitivity analysis}, and calibrate the threshold
  against an observed covariate.
\item
  Run a \textbf{placebo} on a pre-treatment period.
\item
  Say plainly what would break the design --- and what unobservable
  would have to exist.
\end{enumerate}
\end{frame}
