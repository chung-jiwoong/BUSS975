What do you do when you have an important causal question but no valid instrument or quasi-experimental design? This video opens a new series on partial identification and sensitivity analysis in linear regression models. This is a potentially useful alternative when standard identification strategies fall short.

Slides used in the video are available here: https://raw.githack.com/tyleransom/st...
Source code for the slides is here: https://github.com/tyleransom/structu...

We begin by reviewing the core assumptions behind instrumental variables: instrument relevance; the exclusion restriction; and instrument exogeneity. Then we go briefly through three recent papers that raise the bar on what counts as a credible IV analysis:

Mellon (American Journal of Political Science) documents 192 papers using rainfall as an instrument and shows a tangled web of exclusion restriction violations that are collective implied across treatments and outcomes.
Lee et al. (American Economic Review) demonstrates that the conventional first-stage F-statistic threshold of 10 is far too low: correcting for poor coverage of the standard t-test requires either adjusting critical values substantially or achieving a first-stage F-statistic approaching 105.
Young (European Economic Review) shows that IV estimates in AEA-journal papers are highly sensitive to high-leverage observations and non-IID errors, which raises further questions about the credibility of published findings.

Given these limitations, this video presents three potential alternatives to IV estimation:
1. reduce omitted variable bias through careful covariate selection
2. concede that a causal effect cannot be recovered in the given setting
3. use partial identification to report a credible interval of plausible treatment effect values rather than a single point estimate. (The remainder of the series goes through this third approach in detail.)
